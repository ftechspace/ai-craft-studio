# Pacing, Rhythm, Cutting to Music, and Story Structure for Film and Short-Form Video

Research date: 2026-10-10. Platform guidance is dated where the source gives a date. "Inferences" are reasoning or arithmetic built on the cited findings, written as rules an editor agent can use. They are not sourced facts.

## 1. Average shot length (ASL) norms by genre and era

### Takeaway
Hollywood ASL fell from about 10 s in the 1930s and 1940s to under 4 s after 2000. Today's "intensified continuity" style runs about 2–3 s on average. Music videos (about 2 s in one studied case) and TV commercials (under 2 s as of 1991) are faster still. I found no reliable published ASL figures for documentaries, wedding films, or TikTok/Reels/Shorts.

### Cited Findings
**Feature films (Cutting et al. 2011, "Quicker, faster, darker", i-Perception 2(6):569–576; 160 English-language films, 1935–2010, 5 genres)**
- ASL was about **10 s in the 1930s and 1940s** and **below 4 s after 2000**. The decline is log-linear (r = −.75) — [Cutting et al. 2011, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485803/)
- Extremes in the sample: longest ASL *The Seven Year Itch* (1955) **26.2 s**; shortest *Rocky IV* (1985) **2.2 s** — [Cutting et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485803/)
- The decline holds in all five genres (action, adventure, animation, comedy, drama; all r < −.55) — [Cutting et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485803/)
- The average film had about **1,100 shots** (about 165,000 frames) — [Cutting et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485803/)
- **Shorter shots go with more motion inside the shot** (r = −.46 overall). The effect is strongest in action (r = −.54) and comedy (r = −.49), weaker in adventure (r = −.40), and not reliable in drama (r = −.24) or animation. Motion (visual activity) rose steadily over the 75 years. Shots with a moving camera fell from 16% (1959) to 6% (1999) — [Cutting et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485803/)
- Cutting has publicly summarised the trend as about **12 s in 1930 to about 2.5 s "today"**. He attributes it to filmmakers gradually exercising more control over viewer attention, not to MTV: the decline started before MTV and continued at a similar rate after it — [FlowingData summary of Cutting](https://flowingdata.com/2014/09/22/evolution-of-movies/); [TVWriter summary](https://tvwriter.com/how-film-and-tv-audiences-have-changed-in-the-last-100-years/)

**Bordwell on "intensified continuity"**
- The contemporary intensified style averages **"2 or 3 seconds per shot"** and relies on over-the-shoulder shots and singles — [Bordwell, "Intensified continuity revisited" (2007)](https://www.davidbordwell.net/blog/2007/05/27/intensified-continuity-revisited/trackback/)
- Silent American films averaged about **5 s per shot**. Sound slowed cutting down. Around 1916–1918 US films went from an ASL of over 10 s (sometimes 30 s) to **5–6 s or less**, and 4–6 s held through the 1920s — [Bordwell blog](https://davidbordwell.net/blog/?p=2590)
- Dialogue scenes are fast too. In *You've Got Mail* (1998) the dialogue sections average about **4.1–4.3 s**, against much longer takes in *The Shop Around the Corner* (1940) — [Bordwell blog, via search summary](https://www.davidbordwell.net/blog/2007/05/27/intensified-continuity-revisited/trackback/)
- Practitioner limit: Michael Bay found his cut of *The Rock* (1996) too rapid-fire when he screened it on a big screen and had to "de-cut" it — [Artforum on Bordwell](https://www.artforum.com/features/david-bordwell-2-195241/)

**Trailers**
- In 21 trailers (a comedy, drama and action trailer per decade, 1940–2010), shot duration declined steadily from 1960 to 2010, tracking the feature-film decline. Dissolves and fades declined the same way — [Iricinschi & Hu 2014, Proc. CogSci, "Parsing strategies in film trailers"](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/00256713). The abstract gives no per-decade ASL numbers.

**Music videos**
- In a shot-length analysis of Mike Posner's "I Took a Pill in Ibiza" (2016), close shots dominate and the **average shot is about 2 s** — [Pérez Rufí & Rodríguez López 2017, Doxa Comunicación](https://repositorioinstitucional.ceu.es/bitstream/10637/8769/1/Edicion_JPPerez$JRodriguez_Doxa_2017.pdf)
- A 40-video study comparing YouTube's most-viewed videos with IMVDb's "most creative" videos found that the two groups have different ASL values and different patterns of music–image correspondence. The figures were not visible in the abstract — [Guarinos & Sedeño-Valdellós, Revista Latina](https://nuevaepoca.revistalatinacs.org/index.php/revista/article/download/807/1228/3965)

**TV commercials**
- In 1991 the average camera shot in TV commercials lasted **under 2 s**, about half the early-1980s length (MacLachlan & Logan, study of 1978–1991) — [WARC abstract](https://www.warc.com/Content/421e1376-baa5-472f-aa9d-adc58bea5146)
- Spot length: the average US TV ad was **24 s in 2019** (26 s in 2018), and ads of 15 s or less made up 57% of all TV ads (MediaRadar). Commercials under 10 s grew 11.6% year over year — [MediaRadar](https://mediaradar.com/?p=7214). In 1965, 77% of US network commercials were 60 s long; by 2004 it was 7% (Nielsen via WARC) — [WARC](https://www.warc.com/content/article/mediafaq/are-60-second-ads-twice-as-effective-as-30-second-ads/en-gb/82053)

### Inferences
- **Starting ASL targets for an editor agent.** These blend the cited numbers above. Where a figure has no source, it is a judgment call and should be tuned per project:
  - Classical or slow drama, contemplative: 6–12 s (classical-era norm about 10 s)
  - Modern feature drama or dialogue: 3–5 s (*You've Got Mail* dialogue about 4.2 s; post-2000 features under 4 s)
  - Modern action or comedy: 2–3 s (Bordwell's intensified style; *Rocky IV* 2.2 s)
  - Music video and commercial: about 1.5–2.5 s (single music video about 2 s; commercials under 2 s by 1991)
  - Trailers: faster than the film they sell. The trend is documented, but I found no number; under 2 s in the final act is plausible but unsourced
- **Motion rule.** The more motion inside a shot, the shorter it can be (r = −.46). Hold static or low-motion shots longer. Fast-moving shots can be trimmed hard.
- **Screen-size rule.** Content cut for phones tolerates faster cutting than a large screen (see the Bay "de-cut" anecdote). When exporting for cinema or TV, lengthen shots slightly.
- **Use ASL as a feel check, not a target to hit exactly.** The median or distribution matters more than the mean. A 3 s ASL usually mixes many 1–2 s cuts with a few long holds.

### Gaps
- **Documentaries, wedding films, social video (TikTok, Reels, Shorts):** I found no peer-reviewed or reliable industry ASL data. Vendor blogs make claims, but none are traceable.
- Barry Salt's per-decade tables and individual Cinemetrics measurements were not fetched, so there are no per-decade means beyond the "about 10 s" to "under 4 s" endpoints.
- No trailer ASL in seconds. The Iricinschi & Hu full text was not extractable; only the abstract was available.
- Bordwell's often-quoted "1999–2000 films mostly 3–6 s ASL" figure (from *The Way Hollywood Tells It*, 2006) could not be verified. The verified figure is "2–3 s" for the intensified style.

## 2. Varying rhythm: accelerando and decelerando, breathing room, tension and release

### Takeaway
Rhythm is shaped by timing (shot length), pacing (rate of change across shots) and "trajectory phrasing" (how the energy of movement carries across cuts), all aimed at cycles of tension and release (Pearlman). Murch ranks emotion (51%) far above rhythm (10%), so the agent should never break an emotional beat just to keep a rhythm.

### Cited Findings
- Pearlman's definition: rhythm in editing is "time, energy, and movement shaped by **timing, pacing and trajectory phrasing**, for the purpose of creating **cycles of tension and release**" — [Routledge blog on *Cutting Rhythms*](https://blog.routledge.com/?p=946)
- *Cutting Rhythms* chapters: Ch. 3 covers timing, pacing and trajectory phrasing. Ch. 4 covers tension, release and synchronization, with a broadcast-news case study — [O'Reilly listing](https://learning.oreilly.com/library/view/~/9780240810140)
- "Trajectory phrasing" is Pearlman's own term for the flow of movement energy across cuts. It draws on Laban's movement "efforts" (the attitude and intention behind movement) — [Senses of Cinema review (2026)](https://www.sensesofcinema.com/2026/book-reviews/feeling-the-screen-storys-pulse-karen-peralmans-cutting-rhythms-creative-film-editing/); [OSU Screendance review](https://ojs.library.osu.edu/screendance/article/5288/galley/5874/download)
- Murch's Rule of Six, the priorities for an ideal cut: **Emotion 51%, Story 23%, Rhythm 10%, Eye-trace 7%, 2D plane of screen 5%, 3D space of action 4%**. If a cut has to give something up, give up criteria from the bottom of the list first (*In the Blink of an Eye*, 1995) — [UT Austin RTF wiki](https://cloud.wikis.utexas.edu/wiki/spaces/rtf318/pages/80022510/Murch+s+Rule+of+Six); [No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2016/11/6-rules-good-cutting-according-oscar-winning-editor-walter-murch)
- Murch himself treats the percentages as a ranking of priorities, not as measurements — [filmdaft (Dec 2025)](https://filmdaft.com/walter-murchs-rule-of-six-the-editors-formula-for-choosing-the-right-cut/)
- Longer trailer shots made viewers agree more on where segments begin and end. This suggests slower cutting makes structure easier to read — [Iricinschi & Hu 2014](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/00256713)
- Over-cutting gets punished on a big screen (Bay's *The Rock* "de-cut") — [Artforum](https://www.artforum.com/features/david-bordwell-2-195241/)

### Inferences
- **Tension and release pattern.** Shorten shots progressively to build (accelerando, e.g., 3 s → 2 s → 1 s → 0.5 s). Then release on a held shot of 3–6 s, roughly 3–5× the preceding ASL. The contrast creates the effect: a long hold only "lands" after fast cutting, and vice versa.
- **Holding on emotion.** When a shot carries the emotional peak (a reaction or a realisation), let emotion (51%) override rhythm (10%). Hold until the emotion has registered, even if that breaks the musical grid. Cut on the next phrase boundary rather than the next beat.
- **Breathing room.** After a dense information beat (dialogue, text, a reveal), add one "air" shot (landscape, detail, wide) of roughly 1.5–2× the local ASL before the next beat. Comprehension improves with shot length (trailer finding).
- **Motion-matched trajectories.** Carry the direction and energy of movement across the cut (Pearlman's trajectory phrasing). A fast pan out should go into a fast move in, and a slowing gesture into a still frame.
- **Avoid a monotone ASL.** A uniform cut length reads as mechanical. Vary shot lengths within a section, e.g., ±30–50% around the section's ASL. This range is a heuristic, not sourced.

### Gaps
- No quantitative studies found on accelerando and decelerando patterns, or on how long "holding on emotion" should last. The numbers above are heuristics.
- Frame.io Insider and ACE editor interviews were not fetched in this pass.

## 3. Cutting to music: beats, bars, phrases, frame math, and editing music

### Takeaway
Cut on beats or downbeats and change sections on phrase boundaries. Editors widely report that cutting **1–2 frames before** the beat feels more in sync than cutting exactly on it, but this is practitioner lore, not measured research. To shorten music, cut on bar lines (downbeats), crossfade the joins briefly, and make sure the final picture lands on the last musical hit.

### Cited Findings
- The **downbeat** (first and strongest beat of a bar or phrase) gives a cleaner transition point than a random beat — [Splice blog (Mar 2026)](https://spliceapp.com/blog/what-video-editors-optimize-drop-timing-edits); [Artlist](https://artlist.io/blog/how-to-add-music-to-a-video/)
- **Cutting 1–2 frames before the beat** is reported to look more in sync even though it is technically early ("your brain will make them feel in sync") — [Birchtree](https://birchtree.me/blog/your-eyes-ears-and-brain-dont-agree/). Others say exactly on the beat, or one frame either side, sometimes looks best and that it comes down to feel — [Adobe community](https://community.adobe.com/t5/premiere-pro-discussions/adjusting-tempo/m-p/11301404). One editor never found cutting a frame late to look good — [EOSHD forum](https://www.eoshd.com/comments/topic/46696-editing/). Hans Zimmer is reported (second-hand) to say cutting a few frames late can let an edit "breathe" — [EOSHD forum](https://www.eoshd.com/comments/topic/46696-editing/). **These sources conflict, and none is peer-reviewed.**
- Shortening music: count the beats, razor at the bar line, delete the extra measures (e.g., cut an 8-bar section to 4) and ripple-delete the gap. Listen for jumps in beat or tempo at each splice and add a short crossfade, with the Premiere audio-crossfade setting changed to about **4 frames** (default is 1). Make the last picture coincide with the song's final note — [Motion Array](https://blog.motionarray.com/learn/premiere-pro/cut-music-to-video-premiere-pro/)
- Workflow: edit the VO or dialogue first, then lay music under it and move clips to match its bars and phrases. Group shots in phrase-length blocks, e.g., two bars of office b-roll, then one bar (4 beats) of talking head — [Artlist](https://artlist.io/blog/how-to-add-music-to-a-video/)
- A cut placed between beats leaves a half-bar that "sounds wrong", so snap music cuts to detected beats — [Audjust](https://www.audjust.com/tools/beat-precision-cutter)
- Tools: Adobe's Remix analyses a track's structure, finds transition points, creates cuts and crossfades, and fits the music to a target duration, but you still need to listen for jumps. Resolve's Fairlight page has AI-assisted beat-marker detection. CapCut, InShot, VN and Edits offer auto-beat markers that propose cut points around drops. Auto tools are fast but imperfect — [Splice blog (Mar 2026)](https://spliceapp.com/blog/what-editors-allow-cutting-clips-to-music-rhythm); [Splice](https://spliceapp.com/blog/what-video-editors-optimize-drop-timing-edits)
- Cutting on the beat makes a music video feel "more like listening than viewing" (Caston, drawing on Vernallis) — [Caston, Univ. of West London](https://repository.uwl.ac.uk/4706/1/EditingDossiermsmi.2017.6.pdf)
- TikTok's official guidance recommends using sound or music and transitions to hold attention, but gives **no BPM or cut-rate numbers** — [TikTok Ads Help, "Creative best practices for performance ads" (updated June 2025)](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/creative-best-practices)

### Inferences
**BPM-to-frames math.** These are my own computations. Beat = 60 / BPM seconds. A 4/4 bar = 4 beats. Frames = seconds × fps.

| BPM | Beat (s) | Beat @24 fps | @25 fps | @30 fps | Bar 4/4 (s) | 8 bars (s) | 16 bars (s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 60 | 1.000 | 24 | 25 | 30 | 4.00 | 32.0 | 64.0 |
| 80 | 0.750 | 18 | 18.75 | 22.5 | 3.00 | 24.0 | 48.0 |
| 90 | 0.667 | 16 | 16.67 | 20 | 2.67 | 21.3 | 42.7 |
| 100 | 0.600 | 14.4 | 15 | 18 | 2.40 | 19.2 | 38.4 |
| 110 | 0.545 | 13.1 | 13.6 | 16.4 | 2.18 | 17.5 | 34.9 |
| 120 | 0.500 | 12 | 12.5 | 15 | 2.00 | 16.0 | 32.0 |
| 128 | 0.469 | 11.25 | 11.7 | 14.1 | 1.875 | 15.0 | 30.0 |
| 140 | 0.429 | 10.3 | 10.7 | 12.9 | 1.71 | 13.7 | 27.4 |
| 150 | 0.400 | 9.6 | 10 | 12 | 1.60 | 12.8 | 25.6 |
| 174 | 0.345 | 8.3 | 8.6 | 10.3 | 1.38 | 11.0 | 22.1 |

- **Avoid cumulative rounding drift.** Compute each cut frame as `round((t0 + n × 60/BPM) × fps)` from the absolute beat time. Do not add a rounded "frames per beat" value repeatedly; at 128 BPM and 30 fps, 14.06 frames per beat drifts about 1 frame every 16 beats. Better still, use detected beat timestamps, because real recordings drift in tempo.
- **Offset knob.** Make "cut offset relative to beat" a parameter, defaulting to −1 frame (range −2 to +1). At 30 fps, 1–2 frames is 33–67 ms; at 24 fps it is 42–83 ms. Expose the setting rather than hard-coding it, because the sources conflict.
- **Cut density by energy.** Low energy or verse: cut every bar (4 beats) or every 2 bars. Medium: every 2 beats. High or drop: every beat, or in bursts of eighth-note cuts (half a beat; at 120 BPM that is 0.25 s, or 7.5 frames at 30 fps, which is close to the practical minimum shot length). At 120 BPM, cutting every bar gives a 2 s ASL, close to the music-video norm in section 1.
- **Section changes go on phrase boundaries.** Most pop, EDM and stock library music is built in 4-, 8- and 16-bar phrases. This is standard music-structure knowledge; I did not fetch a specific source for it. Put scene or section changes (new location, act change, title card) on the downbeat of a new phrase, and save the biggest visual change (hero shot, logo, reveal) for the drop or the first downbeat after a build.
- **Builds.** During a riser or build, shorten shots progressively (every bar → every 2 beats → every beat → every half-beat). Then on the drop's downbeat either cut to the strongest shot, or hold a long shot over the drop for contrast.
- **Music editing rule set.** (1) Detect beats and downbeats. (2) Cut music only on downbeats, preferably phrase starts. (3) Trim whole bars or whole phrases, never fractions. (4) Crossfade about 2–4 frames (about 4 frames per the Motion Array tip). (5) Keep the song's natural ending, or a final hit, at the end of the picture; it is usually easier to keep the original ending and cut out middle phrases than to fade out. (6) Listen-check every join for doubled transients or broken melody.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study establishing the "1–2 frames early" effect for film cuts. The audiovisual synchrony perception literature (e.g., temporal binding windows) was not fetched.
- Musicbed and Premiere/Resolve official beat-marker documentation were not fetched directly; the tool claims rely on the Splice and Motion Array blogs.
- No sourced data on cut-on-every-beat versus every-bar effectiveness or viewer preference.

## 4. Hooks: the first 1–3 seconds, retention, and opening devices

### Takeaway
Platform guidance converges on getting the hook and value proposition into the **first 3 s** (TikTok: proposition in 3 s, hook within 6 s; Meta/Nielsen 2016: up to 47% of campaign value delivered in 3 s, 74% in 10 s). YouTube measures the "intro" as the **first 30 s**. Many viral-stat claims ("67% better", "2.2× views") trace back to vendor blogs and are unverifiable.

### Cited Findings
- **TikTok (official, updated June 2025):** "Introduce your content proposition in the **first 3 seconds** for better recall and awareness." "Prioritize your hook in the **first 6 seconds** to boost engagement and increase watch time", using suspense, surprise or other emotions. Text overlays at **5–10 words per second**. Vertical **9:16**, at least 720p. Structure: **hook → unique selling points → clear CTA**. A DIY, not-overly-polished style that fits in with user-generated content. Test 3–5 creatives per ad group — [TikTok Ads Help](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/creative-best-practices)
- **TikTok:** "**90% of ad recall impact** is captured within the first six seconds" — [TikTok for Business blog](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en-CA/blog/creative-best-practices-top-performing-ads)
- **Facebook/Nielsen (2016):** **up to 47%** of a video campaign's value comes in the **first 3 s**, and **up to 74%** in the **first 10 s** — [Meta newsroom (Spanish, Feb 2016)](https://about.fb.com/es/news/2016/02/capta-la-atencion-con-nuevas-funcionalidades-para-anuncios-de-video/). Salesforce restates the same research as lift: 3 s of viewing gives 47% of ad-recall lift and 32% of awareness lift; 10 s gives 74% and 65% — [Salesforce 2016](https://salesforce.com/content/blogs/us/en/2016/02/facebook-video-ads-global.html). The figures are about 10 years old, and the two phrasings measure different things.
- **YouTube (Help Center):** the "Intro" key moment is the percentage of viewers still watching **after the first 30 s**. If it is low, YouTube advises matching the opening to the title and thumbnail and trying different styles in the first 30 s. "Top moments" late in a video should be moved earlier, because audience size typically decreases over the length of a video. "Dips" show where viewers abandon or skip. "Spikes" show rewatches, which can also mean a section was unclear — [YouTube Help 9314415](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en); [YouTube Help 1715160](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1715160). A typical intro is about 30 s, and "above typical" intros keep at least half the audience past 30 s (per search summary of the Help page; not verified verbatim). Typical retention is benchmarked against your last 10 videos of similar length — [YouTube Help 1715160](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1715160)
- **Instagram (Jan 2025):** Mosseri raised the Reels maximum from 90 s to **3 min**, saying 90 s was "too short for those who want to share longer stories" — [Deccan Herald](https://www.deccanherald.com/amp/story/technology/instagram-extends-reels-duration-from-90-seconds-to-3-minutes-3363553); [David Zucker Substack (dates it 18 Jan 2025)](https://davidzucker.substack.com/p/three-minute-instagram-reels-have). I found **no official Instagram statement tying hooks to a specific number of seconds**.
- **Vendor claims (unverified, treat as anecdote):** a pain-point question hook beats a generic "Hey guys…" opening by 23% retention; hooks in the first 2 s perform "67% better", attributed to TikTok Creative Center but untraceable; 70–85% three-second hold gives 2.2× views; above 65% three-second retention gives 4–7× impressions — [Opus Pro](https://www.opus.pro/blog/tiktok-hook-formulas); [Teleprompter.com](https://teleprompter.com/blog/tiktok-3-second-rule); [Virlo](https://virlo.ai/blog/why-the-first-few-seconds-matter)
- Recurring practitioner hook types: pattern interrupt, bold claim, question demanding an answer, surprising visual, relatable problem, concrete benefit. Motion in the first 2–3 s. Captions or text, because many viewers scroll with sound off — [Opus Pro](https://www.opus.pro/blog/tiktok-hook-formulas); [Straight North](https://www.straightnorth.com/blog/meta-ads-creative-best-practices-that-convert/)
- Reels ads: a suggested **6–30 s**, 9:16. A 15–30 s story is a good target. Hard-sell openings that look like ads get scrolled past; native, UGC-style content blends in — [JumpFly](https://www.jumpfly.com/blog/reel-talk-a-beginners-guide-to-metas-reels-placement/)

### Inferences
- **Hook rules for the agent:**
  - Frame 0 must already contain motion or a face, never a black or fade-in.
  - The most striking or curiosity-raising shot goes first. This could be a flash-forward to the climax or payoff ("in medias res"), followed by a rewind into the story.
  - The value proposition or question should be legible, as on-screen text, by 3 s.
  - No logo or title card in the first 3 s on social platforms (on cinema or YouTube long-form, a cold open can run 15–30 s before titles).
  - First-shot ASL can be short (0.5–1.5 s), with 2–3 quick cuts in the first 3 s acting as a pattern interrupt.
- **Cold open and flash-forward.** For 60 s or longer pieces, open on a 1–3 s clip of the peak moment, cut to a title or text hook, then start chronologically. This combines the TikTok "hook in 6 s" guidance with the YouTube advice to move top moments earlier.
- **Captions.** Size on-screen text for reading at TikTok's 5–10 words per second. That is fast; for comprehension, 3–4 words per second is safer for non-native or older audiences (heuristic, unsourced).
- **YouTube long-form.** Treat the 30 s mark as the first checkpoint and deliver the promise of the title and thumbnail by then.

### Gaps
- No primary 2024–2026 YouTube Shorts or Instagram document stating a hook window in seconds was found. The YouTube Shorts 3-minute limit (reported October 2024) was not verified in this pass.
- I found no peer-reviewed retention curves for short-form video. TikTok Creative Center data needs interactive browser access.
- The Meta/Nielsen figures date from 2016; I found no updated Meta primary source.

## 5. Story structure for short films, social videos, trailers, and sizzle reels

### Takeaway
Use compressed three-act structures. Trailers segment empirically into about **1/4 exposition, 1/2 complication, 1/4 resolution/climax**. Social ads follow **hook → value/USPs → CTA**. Theatrical trailers run about 1.5–2.5 min and TV spots 15–60 s. End on the strongest image or musical hit.

### Cited Findings
- Viewer segmentation of trailers follows a **1/4 – 1/2 – 1/4** three-act pattern. Drama and action trailers spend roughly half their time on complication — [Iricinschi & Hu 2014](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/00256713)
- Practitioner trailer structure: Act 1 introduces the premise and characters; Act 2 mixes scenes to show conflict and introduce the antagonist; Act 3 shows the most epic and dramatic scenes. The trailer's climax is usually not the film's climax. Teasers often use a 2-act structure. Theatrical trailers run about **1.5–2.5 min** and TV spots **15–60 s**. These are from practitioner guides surfaced in search, not individually fetched — [StudioBinder](https://studiobinder.com/blog/how-to-make-a-movie-trailer); [MasterClass](https://www.masterclass.com/articles/how-to-make-a-movie-trailer); [Filmustage](https://filmustage.com/blog/how-to-craft-an-engaging-movie-trailer/)
- One blog claims an MPAA-mandated average trailer length of 2:30; this could not be verified — [search summary of the practitioner guides above](https://www.nfi.edu/how-to-make-a-movie-trailer/)
- TikTok's ad structure: **hook → unique selling points → clear call-to-action** (June 2025) — [TikTok Ads Help](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/creative-best-practices)
- End the piece so the final picture lands on the song's last note — [Motion Array](https://blog.motionarray.com/learn/premiere-pro/cut-music-to-video-premiere-pro/)
- Spot lengths in market: about 57% of US TV ads are 15 s or shorter; the average is 24 s (2019) — [MediaRadar](https://mediaradar.com/?p=7214). Reels ads: 6–30 s suggested — [JumpFly](https://www.jumpfly.com/blog/reel-talk-a-beginners-guide-to-metas-reels-placement/)

### Inferences
**Time budgets for the agent.** These apply the 1/4–1/2–1/4 split and the hook rule. The ASL ranges are heuristics that taper toward the climax.

| Length | Hook | Act 1 (setup) | Act 2 (build/complication) | Act 3 (climax + button) | Typical ASL |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 15 s | 0–2 s | 2–4 s | 4–11 s | 11–15 s (CTA/logo last 2–3 s) | 0.8–1.5 s |
| 30 s | 0–3 s | 3–8 s | 8–22 s | 22–30 s (button last 2–4 s) | 1–2 s |
| 60 s | 0–3 s | 3–15 s | 15–45 s | 45–60 s (button last 3–5 s) | 1.5–2.5 s |
| 90 s | 0–3 s | 3–22 s | 22–67 s | 67–90 s | 2–3 s |
| 2–3 min (short film, recap, wedding highlight, sizzle) | 0–5 s cold open | first ~25% | middle ~50% | last ~25% | 2–4 s, with 4–8 s emotional holds |

- **Montage beginning, middle and end.** Begin with an establishing or slower phrase (cut every bar). The middle escalates (every 2 beats, then every beat). The climax comes on the drop or final chorus. End on a hero shot held 2–4 s or until the final musical hit and its decay.
- **Endings ("button").** Make the last shot the strongest single image (hero shot), a callback to the opening shot or hook, or a light comic or emotional beat after the climax. It should land on the final downbeat or hit, with any title or logo after the hit. Avoid fading the music out mid-phrase; edit the music so its natural ending matches (see section 3).
- **Trailer and sizzle template.**
  1. Cold open or hook (0–10 s).
  2. Act 1 premise at a slower ASL with dialogue lines.
  3. A "turn" beat, often a music stop or silence, about 1 s of black or a held shot.
  4. Act 2 escalation with progressively shorter shots on the beat.
  5. Act 3 rapid-fire montage on the music's peak.
  6. Title or logo on the final hit.
  7. A post-title button (a short gag or reveal, 3–8 s).
  
  This combines the Iricinschi & Hu proportions with the practitioner act roles above.
- **For 30 s or under, collapse to hook → payoff → CTA.** TikTok's 3-part structure is effectively a 3-act structure at this length.
