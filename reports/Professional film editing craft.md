# Cut for Emotion, Deliver by Numbers

A professional editor runs two systems at once, and an AI editor has to keep them apart. The first is a ranked judgement system. Walter Murch weights a cut's emotional truth at **51%**, story at 23%, rhythm 10%, eye-trace 7%, screen direction 5% and spatial continuity 4%. A cut that is emotionally right may therefore break continuity, but a cut with perfect continuity may never betray the emotion ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2016/11/6-rules-good-cutting-according-oscar-winning-editor-walter-murch)). The second is a compliance system of hard numbers:

- Web and social audio sits near **−14 LUFS integrated with true peak at or below −1 dBTP**. European broadcast is −23 LUFS, US broadcast −24 LKFS, and Netflix −27 LKFS dialogue-gated at −2 dBTP.
- Text stays inside the **90% graphics-safe area**. On 9:16 ads it also stays out of the top 14% and bottom 35%.
- Captions run **at most 42 characters per line at no more than 20 characters per second**.
- Slow motion is conformed at **timeline fps ÷ capture fps**.
- Every skin tone sits on the vectorscope's skin line.

Most day-to-day craft sits between those two systems: J/L-cut offsets, flash-frame lengths, speed-ramp curves, freeze-frame holds and grade slider values. Here the research found no published standard, only consistent practitioner lore. Those values belong in an agent as tunable defaults, checked by watching the result. The evidence also overturns two textbook habits. Viewers prefer match-on-action cuts with a small *gap* of about 4 frames at 24 fps, not the classic overlap. And leading cinematographers keep darker skin dense and warm instead of lifting it toward the 70 IRE once treated as "correct." Genre decides the spine of an edit. Speech-led forms are built audio-first, while montage forms lock the music and its beat grid before any picture is cut. And because slow motion, flashes, fades to black, aspect changes and speed ramps all weaken with repetition, scarcity is the hidden variable behind almost every "premium" technique.

## Emotion outranks continuity in every cut decision

Murch's ideal cut satisfies six criteria at once: emotion, story, rhythm, eye-trace, the two-dimensional plane of the screen, and the three-dimensional space of the action. When it cannot, the editor gives criteria up from the bottom of the list first: "don't ever give up emotion before story… don't give up planarity before spatial continuity" ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2016/11/6-rules-good-cutting-according-oscar-winning-editor-walter-murch)). The weights, **51/23/10/7/5/4**, make emotion worth more than the other five combined ([UT Austin RTF318](https://cloud.wikis.utexas.edu/wiki/spaces/rtf318/pages/80022510/Murch+s+Rule+of+Six)). Murch treats them as a ranking of priorities, not a measurement ([FilmDaft](https://filmdaft.com/walter-murchs-rule-of-six-the-editors-formula-for-choosing-the-right-cut/)). Each higher criterion hides problems in the ones below it, but not the other way round. That is why audiences forgive a mismatched hand position in a moment that lands emotionally. For an agent this becomes a weighted score for each candidate cut frame, plus a hard rule: no gain in a lower criterion, however clean the action match, may cost emotion or story.

Murch treats every cut as a blink. A shot presents an idea, and the cut ends that thought and starts the next. He saw it while editing *The Conversation*, when his cut points kept falling on Gene Hackman's blinks ([Musicbed](https://www.musicbed.com/articles/?p=937)). In practice the cut belongs where a thought completes: after a line's meaning lands, when a listener blinks or shifts expression, or when an action resolves. It does not belong mid-thought. Murch finds the frame in real time, never frame by frame. He plays the clip, stops where the cut feels right, notes the timecode, rewinds and repeats. **Landing on the same frame three times** means he has found the scene's rhythm; scattered results mean he hasn't ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2016/03/intriguing-editing-tips-oscar-winning-editor-walter-murch)). For walking shots he gives the subject two steps and cuts on the uplift of the third ("step, step, cut"), and cuts on the footfall itself only when the cut should be noticed. He also cuts picture with the sound off. When sound goes back on, roughly **70% of cuts are fine, 10% need fixing and 15% turn out better by happy accident**. That supports a two-pass check for an agent: one pass judging faces and eyes in silence, then one pass with sound to catch the cuts that sound breaks.

Continuity editing hides the cut with a small grammar:

- **The 180° rule** keeps the camera on one side of the axis of action, so screen direction and eyelines stay consistent. The line can be crossed legitimately through a neutral on-axis shot, a cutaway, an insert, a new motion vector, or a shot in which the camera visibly moves across it ([Indiana University C229](https://jkmedia.sitehost.iu.edu/C229/Lecture9_sp25.docx)).
- **The 30° rule** requires at least that much change of angle between consecutive shots of the same subject. Anything smaller reads as a jump cut ([Wolfcrow](https://wolfcrow.com/the-15-essential-rules-of-film-continuity/)).
- **Eyeline matches** pair a looking character with what they see, and depend on the 180° rule to point the glance the right way.
- **Cutting in movement:** Edward Dmytryk's rule, "whenever possible, cut 'in movement'," hides the cut inside motion ([Hecht & Kalkofen 2009](https://experimental.psychologie.uni-mainz.de/files/2024/06/2009hecht-kalkofen.pdf)).

How much action to overlap at a match cut is one of the rare craft questions with controlled evidence, and the evidence contradicts the manuals. Dmytryk prescribed a 3–5-frame overlap and Anderson about 2 frames. Reisz and Millar required the moving object to sit in exactly the same position on both sides of the cut. Hecht and Kalkofen's observers preferred a **gap averaging 182 ms, "leaving 4 frames on the cutting floor" at 24 fps**, and the preference held whether the angle changed by 10° or 130° ([Hecht & Kalkofen 2009](https://experimental.psychologie.uni-mainz.de/files/2024/06/2009hecht-kalkofen.pdf)). Changes in shot size moved the preference:

- **Close to wide ("enlargement")** produced the largest preferred gaps, **up to about 350 ms (≈8 frames at 24 fps)**.
- **Large wide-to-close cuts ("contraction")** favoured an overlap.

Most of the practising cutters the researchers interviewed agreed that dropping a few frames smooths the transition. Viewers' tolerance was huge (SD ≈ 797 ms), and the stimuli were animated objects rather than actors. A sensible agent default follows from this:

1. Find the straight match on the clearest mid-motion frame.
2. Try trimming 2–4 frames off the head of the incoming shot.
3. Trim more when cutting close to wide. Use no trim, or a slight overlap, when cutting wide to close.
4. Let emotion and eye-trace settle close calls.

Pacing inside a scene is an emotional choice too. On *Dune*, Joe Walker let moments play in long holds, such as a 17-second desert plate and a long pause before Stilgar spits, rather than breaking them up with reaction shots. He required almost every shot to be narratively driven rather than a "beauty shot," and often built a scene backward from the close-up that carried its key emotional beat ([Frame.io](https://blog.frame.io/2021/10/27/art-of-the-cut-dune-joe-walker/)).

### Cut types split into invisible and expressive families

Invisible cuts hide the edit. In a **J-cut** the next shot's audio arrives before its picture. In an **L-cut** the previous shot's audio carries over the next picture, for example a line echoing over the following scene ([Storyblocks](https://www.storyblocks.com/resources/tutorials/film-editing-techniques)). Split edits are the main tool for natural dialogue: in real conversation we hear a reply before we turn to look, and we keep watching a speaker after they stop. Expressive cuts make the audience feel the edit:

- **Match cut:** a hard cut that links two shots by shape, colour or movement, often across a leap in time. The canonical example is the bone-to-spacecraft cut in *2001* ([Filmsupply](https://wpengine.fm.co/filmsupply/?p=3626)).
- **Smash cut:** an abrupt, unprepared cut used for comedic deflation, horror and drama. Recurring patterns include broken promise, danger to safety, praise to reversal, and victory to grief ([StudioBinder](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/what-is-a-smash-cut-definition/)).
- **Cross-cutting:** alternates locations so that actions feel simultaneous.
- **Jump cut:** a continuity violation that compresses time. It is accepted grammar in short-form talking heads, where it removes dead air and adds energy, though too many feel choppy ([Virlo](https://virlo.ai/blog/how-do-influencers-edit-their-videos)).

The default for narrative dialogue and documentary interviews is invisible. Expressive cuts should be deliberate, motivated and rare enough to keep their force.

The Kuleshov effect explains why reaction shots carry so much weight. One neutral close-up of Ivan Mozzhukhin, cut against a bowl of soup, a girl in a coffin and a woman on a divan, was read as hunger, grief and desire ([Wikipedia: Kuleshov effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuleshov_effect)). The replication record is mixed but leans positive:

- Prince and Hensley (1992) failed to replicate it with 137 participants.
- Mobbs et al. (2006) found it with fMRI.
- Barratt et al. (2016) supported it across 24 sequences.
- Baranowski and Hecht (2017) showed that **music alone shifts how a face is read**.

For an agent this means a reaction shot cannot be scored in isolation. Its meaning depends on the preceding shot and on the score, so selection has to be judged in sequence.

Eisenstein's five montage methods run from mechanical to conceptual ([StudioBinder](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/soviet-montage-theory/); [PremiumBeat](https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/soviet-montage-theory-types)):

| Method | What drives the cut | Modern use |
|---|---|---|
| Metric | Fixed frame counts, like a musical measure | Cutting on the beat or bar |
| Rhythmic | Movement inside each shot | Cutting to on-screen motion |
| Tonal | Mood: light, focus, sound, shape | Sequencing shots by brightness, colour or energy |
| Overtonal | All three combined | Layered sequences |
| Intellectual | Collision of unrelated images producing an idea | Argument by juxtaposition in documentaries and commercials |

### Transitions carry meaning, so the hard cut is the default

*Grammar of the Edit* names four transitions: cut, dissolve, wipe and fade. Each "carries with it its own meaning," and the cut is the most common ([O'Reilly: Grammar of the Edit ch. 4](https://oreilly.com/library/view/grammar-of-the/9780240521206/06_Chapter4.html)). A dissolve implies continuity and, usually, passing time. A fade to black marks "closure, finality, or separation," is "most effective when reserved for moments that truly warrant emphasis," and feels more reflective the longer it runs ([HowToFilmSchool](https://howtofilmschool.com/dictionary/fade-to-black/)). Little authoritative guidance exists on wipes, so they are best kept for deliberate stylisation. Star wipes, page curls and glitter flares read as amateur unless the retro look is the point ([Film Editing Pro](https://www.filmeditingpro.com/?p=24508)).

| Situation | Transition | Duration (24 fps) | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Same scene, continuous time | Hard cut | — | [Grammar of the Edit](https://oreilly.com/library/view/grammar-of-the/9780240521206/06_Chapter4.html) |
| Time passing, change of place, flashback | Dissolve | 24–48 frames (1–2 s) | [Wikipedia: Dissolve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolve_%28filmmaking%29) |
| Softening a jump cut or a startling hard cut | Short "soft cut" dissolve | 6–12 frames | [Wikipedia: Dissolve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissolve_%28filmmaking%29) |
| House-style fades (university standard) | Fade or dissolve | 2 or 8 frames; 12 or 18 for slower edits (3/10 and 15/20 at 30 fps) | [Boston University](https://www.bu.edu/brand/files/2019/06/bu-video-branding-standards.pdf) |
| Long time gap versus a small change | Fade-out/fade-in pair versus dissolve | — | [Boris FX](https://borisfx.com/blog/how-does-a-dissolve-differ-from-a-fade-transition/) |
| End of scene, act or film | Fade to black | Longer reads as more emotional | [HowToFilmSchool](https://howtofilmschool.com/dictionary/fade-to-black/) |
| Memory, transcendence, ambiguity | Fade to white | — | [HowToFilmSchool](https://howtofilmschool.com/dictionary/fade-to-black/) |
| Deliverable head and tail | Black | 2 s each | [Boston University](https://www.bu.edu/brand/files/2019/06/bu-video-branding-standards.pdf) |

## Shot lengths fell from 12 seconds to under 4, and music now sets the grid

Average shot length (ASL) is the clearest numeric fingerprint of pacing. Across 160 English-language films from 1935 to 2010, ASL fell from **about 10 s in the 1930s and 1940s to below 4 s after 2000**. The decline was log-linear and appeared in every genre studied ([Cutting et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485803/)). Cutting attributes it to filmmakers gradually taking more control of viewer attention, not to MTV, because the decline began well before 1982 and continued at the same rate afterwards ([FlowingData](https://flowingdata.com/2014/09/22/evolution-of-movies/)). David Bordwell puts today's "intensified continuity" at **2–3 seconds per shot**. Even dialogue runs fast: the dialogue scenes of *You've Got Mail* average about 4.1–4.3 s ([Bordwell](https://www.davidbordwell.net/blog/2007/05/27/intensified-continuity-revisited/trackback/)).

| Form | Reference ASL | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Hollywood, 1930s–40s | ~10 s | [Cutting et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485803/) |
| Features after 2000 | under 4 s | [Cutting et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485803/) |
| Intensified continuity | 2–3 s | [Bordwell](https://www.davidbordwell.net/blog/2007/05/27/intensified-continuity-revisited/trackback/) |
| Modern dialogue (*You've Got Mail*) | 4.1–4.3 s | [Bordwell](https://www.davidbordwell.net/blog/2007/05/27/intensified-continuity-revisited/trackback/) |
| Sample extremes | 26.2 s (*The Seven Year Itch*) to 2.2 s (*Rocky IV*) | [Cutting et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485803/) |
| Music video ("I Took a Pill in Ibiza") | ~2 s | [Pérez Rufí & Rodríguez López 2017](https://repositorioinstitucional.ceu.es/bitstream/10637/8769/1/Edicion_JPPerez$JRodriguez_Doxa_2017.pdf) |
| TV commercials, 1991 | under 2 s (half the early-1980s figure) | [WARC](https://www.warc.com/Content/421e1376-baa5-472f-aa9d-adc58bea5146) |

Three findings turn these numbers into rules.

- **Motion licenses short shots.** Shorter shots correlate with more motion inside the frame (r = −.46 overall, strongest in action and comedy, unreliable in drama) ([Cutting et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485803/)). Static, low-motion shots should be held longer, and fast-moving shots can be trimmed hard.
- **Screen size matters.** Michael Bay found his cut of *The Rock* too rapid-fire on a big screen and had to "de-cut" it ([Artforum](https://www.artforum.com/features/david-bordwell-2-195241/)). Phone-sized delivery tolerates faster cutting than cinema.
- **Slower cutting reads more clearly.** Longer trailer shots made viewers agree more on where segments began and ended ([Iricinschi & Hu 2014](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/00256713)).

The often-quoted cut rates of "around twenty-five cuts a minute" for action and six for "normal" American dialogue ([Musicbed](https://www.musicbed.com/articles/?p=937)) are not reliably Murch's own words. Six cuts a minute is also a 10 s ASL, far slower than Bordwell's measured 4.2 s (about 14 cuts a minute), so it is best read as a historical floor. No reliable ASL data exists for documentaries, wedding films or TikTok, Reels and Shorts. Vendor claims for these forms are not traceable.

Karen Pearlman defines editing rhythm as "time, energy, and movement shaped by **timing, pacing and trajectory phrasing**, for the purpose of creating **cycles of tension and release**" ([Routledge](https://blog.routledge.com/?p=946)). Trajectory phrasing is her term for carrying the energy of movement across cuts. Tension and release work by contrast: a long hold lands only after fast cutting, and fast cutting excites only after stillness. Because Murch ranks emotion at 51% and rhythm at 10%, a shot carrying an emotional peak should be held until the emotion registers, even if that breaks the musical grid. The cut then moves to the next phrase boundary instead of the next beat.

### Beats, bars and phrases convert directly into frames

Cuts land best on downbeats, the first and strongest beat of a bar, and section changes land best on phrase boundaries ([Artlist](https://artlist.io/blog/how-to-add-music-to-a-video/)). Whether to cut exactly on the beat is disputed:

- Editors report that cutting **1–2 frames before** the beat feels more in sync ([Birchtree](https://birchtree.me/blog/your-eyes-ears-and-brain-dont-agree/)).
- Others say exactly on the beat or one frame either side, depending on feel ([Adobe Community](https://community.adobe.com/t5/premiere-pro-discussions/adjusting-tempo/m-p/11301404)).
- One editor has never seen a late cut look good ([EOSHD](https://www.eoshd.com/comments/topic/46696-editing/)).
- A beat-sync vendor cuts on the frame containing the transient, or the frame after it when the beat falls between frames ([Framia](https://framia.converge.ai/page/en-US/blog/beat-sync-ai-music-video)).
- Cutting just before or after the beat is also used deliberately to create tension or surprise ([VEGAS](https://www.vegascreativesoftware.com/in/post-production/how-to-edit-video-footage-to-the-beats-of-music)).

None of these sources is peer-reviewed. An agent should therefore expose a "beat offset" parameter, defaulting to −1 frame with a range of −2 to +1, rather than hard-coding one answer. The tempo math is exact: one beat lasts 60 ÷ BPM seconds and one 4/4 bar lasts 240 ÷ BPM seconds. The table is derived from those formulas.

| BPM | Beat (s) | Frames/beat @24 | @25 | @30 | 4/4 bar (s) | 8 bars (s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 60 | 1.000 | 24 | 25 | 30 | 4.00 | 32.0 |
| 80 | 0.750 | 18 | 18.75 | 22.5 | 3.00 | 24.0 |
| 90 | 0.667 | 16 | 16.7 | 20 | 2.67 | 21.3 |
| 100 | 0.600 | 14.4 | 15 | 18 | 2.40 | 19.2 |
| 120 | 0.500 | 12 | 12.5 | 15 | 2.00 | 16.0 |
| 128 | 0.469 | 11.25 | 11.7 | 14.06 | 1.875 | 15.0 |
| 140 | 0.429 | 10.3 | 10.7 | 12.9 | 1.71 | 13.7 |
| 174 | 0.345 | 8.3 | 8.6 | 10.3 | 1.38 | 11.0 |

Two engineering rules follow.

- **Compute each cut from absolute beat time.** Calculate every cut as round((t0 + n × 60/BPM) × fps). Adding a rounded frames-per-beat value repeatedly drifts: at 128 BPM and 30 fps, 14.06 frames per beat slips a frame every 16 beats. Detected beat timestamps are better still, because real recordings drift in tempo.
- **Bar length is the natural music-video ASL.** An analysis of music videos found the dominant shot lengths often equal one bar of the song: about **2.72 s at 88 BPM and 1.93 s at 124 BPM**. About a fifth of clips showed bar-level synchronisation ([arXiv 2108.00970](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.00970)).

Uniform shot lengths let viewers anticipate the cut, so lengths should vary, with some cuts on secondary beats as well as downbeats ([No Film School / Larry Jordan](https://nofilmschool.com/2014/09/larry-jordan-teaches-us-how-create-video-montage-set-music)). In 4/4 the default is to cut on the beat, with occasional two-beat or two-bar cuts to break the monotony ([Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/node/4905278)). When voice-over or dialogue exists, edit it first, then lay the music under it and group shots in phrase-length blocks ([Artlist](https://artlist.io/blog/how-to-add-music-to-a-video/)).

To shorten a track:

1. Count beats and razor on bar lines.
2. Delete whole measures, for example cutting an 8-bar section to 4, and ripple the gap closed.
3. Crossfade each splice. Motion Array suggests raising Premiere's audio crossfade from 1 frame to about **4 frames**.
4. Make the final picture land on the song's last note ([Motion Array](https://blog.motionarray.com/learn/premiere-pro/cut-music-to-video-premiere-pro/)).

To keep a track's real ending, cut on the beat just before the natural end and join with a short crossfade. If it sounds wrong, move the edit back 4 bars, and make sure the final downbeat lands after all dialogue ([Storyblocks](https://www.storyblocks.com/resources/?p=25313)).

### Hooks are judged in the first 3 seconds and the first 30

Platform guidance agrees on a short window. TikTok's official ad guidance (updated June 2025) says to introduce the content proposition "in the **first 3 seconds**," put the hook "in the **first 6 seconds**," keep text overlays at **5–10 words per second**, and structure ads as hook → unique selling points → clear CTA ([TikTok Ads Help](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/creative-best-practices)). TikTok also says **90% of ad recall impact** is captured within the first six seconds ([TikTok for Business](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en-CA/blog/creative-best-practices-top-performing-ads)). Meta's 2016 Nielsen work found up to **47%** of a campaign's value in the first 3 s and up to **74%** in the first 10 s ([Meta](https://about.fb.com/es/news/2016/02/capta-la-atencion-con-nuevas-funcionalidades-para-anuncios-de-video/)). Those figures are a decade old.

YouTube measures the "Intro" as the share of viewers still watching after **30 s**. If it is low, YouTube advises matching the opening to the title and thumbnail. It also suggests moving late "top moments" earlier, because audience size usually declines through a video ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en)). Retention strategist Mario Joos argues that "It's not the cutting speed, it's the speed at which you introduce progressive novel information." He recommends showing value within 15 s and adding new information every **10–15 s**, and calls 70% retention "the biggest myth" ([Vidpros](https://vidpros.com/retention-editing-what-you-can-learn-from-mario-joos/)). Viral statistics such as "hooks in 2 s perform 67% better" trace back to untraceable vendor blogs and should not be used.

For an agent the operational rules are:

- Frame 0 already contains motion or a face, never black or a fade-in.
- The most striking shot, or a flash-forward to the payoff, opens the piece.
- The proposition is legible by 3 s.
- No logo card comes first on social platforms.

### Story structure compresses to hook, complication and payoff

Viewers segment trailers in a **¼–½–¼ pattern** of exposition, complication and climax ([Iricinschi & Hu 2014](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/00256713)). Theatrical trailers run about 1.5–2.5 min and TV spots 15–60 s ([StudioBinder](https://studiobinder.com/blog/how-to-make-a-movie-trailer)). The average US TV ad lasted 24 s in 2019, and 57% ran 15 s or less ([MediaRadar](https://mediaradar.com/?p=7214)). Applied to a 60 s piece, that proportion gives about 15 s of setup, 30 s of escalation and 15 s of climax and button, with the hook inside the first 3 s.

At 30 s or less the structure collapses into TikTok's hook → value → CTA. Endings land on the strongest image, a callback to the opening, or the final musical hit, with any logo after the hit.

## Picture lock is the gate everything downstream waits on

The professional pipeline runs ingest and verified backup → organisation → logging and selects → stringouts → assembly → rough cut → fine cut → **picture lock** → turnover to sound and colour → finishing → deliverables.

- **Assembly** follows the script, includes every line, and is "sometimes more than twice the length of the final film." Its main value is catching unusable shots while reshoots are still possible ([Frame.io Workflow Guide](https://workflow.frame.io/guide/editing-stages)).
- **Rough cut** is usually the longest stage. One estimate is "up to 10 weeks or more for a feature" ([Macmillan *Filmmaking*](https://digfir-published.macmillanusa.com/filmmaking1e/filmmaking1e_ch12_2.html)).
- **Fine cut** works shot by shot after the rough cut has fixed structure ([Cutback](https://cutback.video/blog/assembly-cut-vs.-rough-cut-what-s-the-difference)).
- **Picture lock** means "no further editorial changes are allowed." Sound and colour are cut to the frame, so later changes "cause issues and extra costs" ([Frame.io Workflow Guide](https://workflow.frame.io/guide/editing-stages)).

A stringout is "a sequence of clips, with light trimming, laid out in linear order." Narrative stringouts are ordered by scene, documentary stringouts by topic or subject ([Cutback](https://cutback.video/blog/stringout-vs-selects-the-ultimate-guide-to-modern-video-prep-%28with-ai-tools%29)). Versioning is universal practice: duplicate and version sequences at each stage ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2015/11/how-cut-short-documentary-films-part-two-building-story-adding-b-roll)). Walker's *Dune* reached at least a 25th fine cut, with bin comments updated at every change ([Frame.io](https://blog.frame.io/2021/10/27/art-of-the-cut-dune-joe-walker/)).

Offline editing runs on proxies, and the proxies must be built to relink cleanly:

- **Codec.** Use intraframe ProRes, DNxHR or CineForm, never Long-GOP H.264 ([Elements.tv](https://elements.tv/blog/everything-you-need-to-know-about-the-proxy-workflow-in-adobe-premiere-pro/)).
- **Matching.** Frame size may divide evenly (960×540 for 1080p), but frame rate, duration and fielding must match the camera originals ([Larry Jordan](https://larryjordan.com/?p=144414)).
- **Conform.** Conform rebuilds the timeline from the original camera files through an EDL, which carries clip names and timecodes, or an XML, which can also carry effects and transforms ([Frame.io Insider](https://blog.frame.io/category/workflow/conform)). Stripping metadata or **changing the frame rate** can break the relink ([OWC](https://www.owc.com/ca/blog/online-vs-offline-editing-why-you-still-need-to-consider-proxy-videos)).
- **Presets.** Premiere's ProRes proxy preset resolutions differ between sources and versions, so check them rather than assume.

Turnover to sound follows the facility's own document "to the letter." The common requirements are:

- **AAF rather than OMF.** OMF caps embedded audio at 2 GB and loses Premiere clip gain ([Production Expert](https://www.production-expert.com/home-page/2020/8/27/aaf-and-omf-from-video-editors-how-to-make-sure-what-they-provide-works-for-us)).
- **Handles.** Specs range from **2 s** ([Todd-AO](https://toddao.com/turnover_specs.pdf)) through 75 frames to a "minimum of 5 s, 10 s preferred" ([IRPR](https://sounddesign.irpr.agency/guides/how-to-prepare-files-for-audio-post-production/)).
- **Sample rate.** Native rate, usually 48 kHz.
- **Reference video.** A reference that exactly matches the locked cut, with burned-in timecode. Drop-frame 29.97 is marked with a semicolon.
- **EDLs.** Separate audio and video EDLs ([Henri Rapp](https://henrirapp.com/preparing-delivery-to-audio-post-production/)).

Footage volume sets the review burden. Shooting ratios typically run:

| Production | Shooting ratio |
|---|---|
| Animation | near 1:1 |
| Controlled narrative | 2:1–4:1 |
| Film-stock productions | 6:1–10:1 |
| Documentary and reality | 20:1 to over 100:1 |

Some famous cases: Hitchcock worked at about 3:1, *Apocalypse Now* at 95:1, *Primer* at 1.04:1 ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2016/03/shooting-ratios-mad-max-fury-road-primer-hitchcock); [Wikipedia: Shooting ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shooting_ratio)), and *Mad Max: Fury Road* at about 240:1 ([Vashi Visuals](https://vashivisuals.com/shooting-ratios-of-feature-films/)). A brief of 40 minutes of raw footage for a 1-minute piece is a normal 40:1 documentary- or commercial-style ratio.

Industry blogs put editing time at 20–30 minutes per finished minute for simple talking heads, 45–60 minutes for a typical video, 1–2 hours for stylised work, and up to 2–10 hours at the extreme. Raw footage volume is a major driver ([TastyEdits](https://www.tastyedits.com/how-long-does-it-take-to-edit-a-video/); [VeedYou](https://www.veedyou.com/how-long-does-it-take-to-edit-a-video/)). The efficient method is selects-first. Make one disciplined viewing pass that marks and rates usable moments; reviewing 40 minutes at 2× takes 20 minutes. Interviews are pulled from a time-coded transcript, then cut only from selects and stringouts ([Frame.io text-based editing](https://blog.frame.io/2023/10/16/premiere-pro-text-based-editing/); [NYFA](https://bk.nyfa.edu/student-resources/how-to-edit-an-interview-for-a-documentary)).

## Real frames beat invented ones in every speed change

The clean way to make slow motion is to *conform*: interpret a high-frame-rate clip at the timeline rate so every output frame is a real captured frame. The arithmetic below is derived, not sourced:

- **Conform speed % = timeline fps ÷ capture fps × 100.**
- **Slowdown factor = capture fps ÷ timeline fps.**
- **Source frames advanced per output frame, k = (capture ÷ timeline) × (speed ÷ 100).**

How a given k plays back:

- **k = 1:** perfectly smooth.
- **k a whole number of 2 or more:** clean fast motion.
- **k = 1/n:** each frame is held n times, giving stepped motion that needs blending or optical flow.
- **Anything else:** an uneven repeat-and-skip pattern, i.e. judder. For example, 59.94 fps footage set to 50% on a 23.976 timeline gives k = 1.25.

The same speed in percent can therefore be clean or juddery depending on the source rate. That is why an agent should interpret clips to the timeline rate rather than type a percentage.

| Capture → timeline | Conform speed | Slowdown | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 59.94 → 29.97 | 50% | 2× | clean |
| 59.94 → 23.976 | 40% | 2.5× | clean only as a conform; 50% judders |
| 119.88 → 29.97 | 25% | 4× | clean |
| 119.88 → 23.976 | 20% | 5× | clean |
| 239.76 → 23.976 | 10% | 10× | clean |
| 50 → 25 / 100 → 25 / 200 → 25 | 50% / 25% / 12.5% | 2× / 4× / 8× | clean (PAL) |
| 120 (integer) → 23.976 | 19.98% | 5.005× | mixed families; conform by interpreting |
| 29.97 → 23.976 | 80% | 1.25× | subtle "dreamy" slow motion |
| 25 → 23.976 | 95.9% | 1.043× | audio drops ~0.7 semitone if uncorrected |

NTSC rates are exact fractions (23.976 = 24000/1001, 59.94 = 60000/1001). Ratios within one family are exact, while mixing integer and /1001 rates gives values near 99.9%. Going slower than the conform limit forces interpolation.

**Frame Sampling** duplicates or drops whole frames. It suits speed-ups and true conforms ([Aiarty](https://www.aiarty.com/edit-video/premiere-pro-time-interpolation.htm)). **Frame Blending** composites neighbouring frames with ghosting and suits mild slow motion of about **70–90%** or a dreamy look. **Optical Flow** synthesises frames from motion vectors. It works best on high-contrast motion at **50% speed or faster**. It warps where similar colours meet or objects occlude one another, and at 20% or slower it is inventing most of the frames. Resolve offers Nearest, Frame Blend and Optical Flow, plus AI Speed Warp in the Studio version. When two elements cross or the camera moves unpredictably, Frame Blend can look better than flow, and heavy retimes may need render-in-place to preview ([Tuts+](https://photography.tutsplus.com/tutorials/how-to-create-slow-motion-in-davinci-resolve-with-optical-flow--cms-106752)). Premiere users have reported for years that Optical Flow and Frame Blending are ignored inside time-remap ramp transitions. Adobe staff could not reproduce the problem, so it is unresolved and worth testing ([Adobe Community](https://community.adobe.com/t5/premiere-pro-bugs/optical-flow-and-frame-blending-haven-t-worked-on-speed-ramps-for-about-7-years/idc-p/15460106)).

Shutter angle decides how a ramp looks at full speed. A 180° shutter is 1/(2 × fps), so 120 fps calls for 1/240 s, and high-frame-rate shooting needs more light ([Stocksy](https://support.stocksy.com/hc/en-us/articles/360049933392)). Played back at real time in a 24p ramp, that 1/240 s exposure behaves like a **36° shutter** (180 × 24/120), so the real-time sections strobe. One editor saw "extreme strobing of the light sources" after interpreting 120 fps footage as 23.976 ([DVinfo](https://www.dvinfo.net/forum/archive/index.php/t-515430.html)). Some practitioners shoot high frame rates at 360° when heavy ramps are planned ([Blackmagic Forum](https://forum.blackmagicdesign.com/viewtopic.php?p=487822)). Others add synthetic motion blur to the fast sections ([Creative Video Tips](https://creativevideotips.com/tutorials/speed-ramp-with-motion-blur-in-davinci-resolve)). Mains-powered lights flicker at high frame rates unless the shutter is matched to the 50 or 60 Hz supply.

A speed ramp is a time-remap curve, normally 100% → slow → 100%, with Bezier easing on every transition. In Premiere you right-click the fx badge and choose Time Remapping > Speed, Ctrl/Cmd-click to add keyframes, drag a segment up or down, and split keyframes into gradual transitions ([Pixflow](https://pixflow.net/blog/how-to-speed-ramp-in-premiere-pro-a-step-by-step-tutorial-for-stunning-video-effects/)). A conformed high-frame-rate clip first has to be pushed to about **250–300%** to play at real time ([Motion Array](https://motionarray.com/premiere-pro/premiere-pro-speed-ramping-tutorial/)), which is the arithmetic inverse of 40%. The craft rule is to ramp into a hit with slow motion and snap back to speed after the impact. The slowest frame should land on the action peak and the music accent. One worked example at about 30 fps runs 100% until frame 20, eases to **25% by frame 28** (on the beat), then returns to **100% by frame 40**: an 8-frame ramp in and a 12-frame ramp out ([AEJuice](https://aejuice.com/blog/how-to-speed-ramp-footage-in-after-effects/)). The best practice for the slow plateau is to sit exactly at the conform speed, 40% for 59.94 → 23.976, so it uses only real frames.

Premiere's own users call its ramp tooling "very outdated" because it lacks built-in easing presets and motion blur ([Adobe Community](https://community.adobe.com/t5/premiere-pro-ideas/revamp-the-speedramp/idi-p/15243579)). A fast-slow-fast ramp with a whoosh on every transition now reads as a late-2010s travel cliché.

Slow motion earns its place on "fast motion or intense emotions": laughing, crying, running, splashing, and story turning points. Used everywhere, it goes dull, and "if changing the frame rate adds nothing, skip it" ([PremiumBeat](https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/tips-for-better-slow-motion-video)). Rarity gives it power. If only the crushing blow is slowed, the impact is "monumental" ([StudioBinder](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/how-to-use-slow-motion-in-film-to-create-iconic-moments/)). It should never sit on lip-synced speech.

Audio under slow motion should be replaced, not stretched. Camera sound recorded at high frame rates "becomes indistinguishable and just quite muddy" ([RouteNote](https://licensing.routenote.com/blog/?p=7109)), and most slow-motion sound in finished films is designed ([DIY Photography](https://www.diyphotography.net/slow-motion-sound-in-videos-is-fake-well-most-of-it-is/)). NLE pitch correction can sound "harsh, metallic," and slowed dialogue is better treated as ADR ([DVinfo](https://www.dvinfo.net/forum/archive/index.php/t-537570.html)). Designed slow-motion sound is a downward whoosh or swell timed "so the sweep bottoms out exactly as the shot reaches its slowest point" ([Morphic](https://morphic.com/resources/sounds/slow-motion-sound-effects)). The pitch-dropped bullet-time sound is fine as a deliberate effect. The research team also infers that a real-time ambience bed should run unbroken under any ramp so the world doesn't sonically change speed. No source states this directly.

Freeze frames serve four jobs:

- character introductions, such as the name cards in *Snatch* or the narrator-pause freeze in *The Wolf of Wall Street* ([StudioBinder](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/what-is-a-freeze-frame-the-best-examples-why-they-work/));
- "photo taken" moments marked by a shutter click;
- comic or narrative pauses;
- endings.

Pick the frame for sharpness, absence of motion blur, peak expression, composition and grain consistency ([Vizard](https://vizard.ai/knowledge-base/video-production/what-is-a-freeze-frame-effect)). Under the freeze, audio can continue, fade, switch to narration or cut abruptly. A "crisp shutter click" turns the held frame into a photograph ([Morphic](https://morphic.com/resources/sounds/camera-shutter-sound-effect)). In Premiere, Insert Frame Hold Segment freezes a section while keeping the rest of the clip ([Storyblocks](https://www.storyblocks.com/resources/tutorials/how-to-freeze-frame-premiere)). Overuse makes the device gimmicky ([HowToFilmSchool](https://howtofilmschool.com/dictionary/freeze-frame/)). No source gives standard hold durations. A narrated freeze lasts as long as its line, and a name-card freeze should satisfy the reading-time rules covered in the titles section.

## Balance first, look last: the colour pipeline

Every source agrees on the order of operations. Correction makes footage neutral and consistent, and only then does grading add a look:

1. Input transform for log footage.
2. White balance, Adobe's "common first step," verified on the RGB parade rather than by eye ([Adobe](https://www.adobe.com/learn/premiere-pro/web/correct-skin-tones)).
3. Exposure and grey scale, then hue and saturation ([Larry Jordan](https://larryjordan.com/articles/color-correction-make-people-look-normal/)).
4. Contrast and saturation.
5. Shot matching.
6. Creative look.
7. Secondaries and vignette.

Order matters because each operation feeds the next ([Pomfort](https://pomfort.com/article/order-of-operations-for-creating-looks-on-set/)). Stylised looks like bleach bypass come after "white balance and contrast correction first" ([Boris FX](https://blog.imagineersystems.com/blog/bleach-bypass-in-davinci-resolve-step-by-step-guide)). Feature colourist Cullen Kelly makes exposure the first adjustment on every shot and works in a fixed node tree. His philosophy is to "establish a strong global look, then adjust only what needs it," giving each shot a "presumption of innocence" ([Mixing Light](https://mixinglight.com/color-grading-tutorials/inside-the-grade-the-first-pass-on-a-feature)). In Premiere terms, that means corrections per clip and the look on an adjustment layer above the scene.

Lumetri adjustments stack and can be tuned independently ([Adobe](https://www.adobe.com/learn/premiere-pro/web/create-color-look-video)). Two controls deserve defaults:

- **Vibrance** boosts muted colours while protecting skin, so it is preferable to Saturation when people are in frame ([Bucknell](https://colorandlight.blogs.bucknell.edu/2018/04/11/color-lesson-2-color-grading/)).
- **Creative Look Intensity** should be expected to come down, because preset looks are "a little on the extreme side" when first applied ([Bucknell](https://colorandlight.blogs.bucknell.edu/2018/04/11/color-lesson-2-color-grading/)).

### Scopes turn "looks right" into numbers

Each scope has a job ([Larry Jordan](https://larryjordan.com/articles/color-correction-make-people-look-normal/); [Adobe](https://www.adobe.com/learn/premiere-pro/web/correct-skin-tones)):

- **Waveform:** exposure and range. Shadows sit at 0–33%, midtones at 33–66% and highlights at 66–100%.
- **RGB parade:** white balance. A neutral shows three equal channel traces.
- **Vectorscope:** hue and saturation. Neutrals collapse to the centre and skin runs along the skin line at roughly **10:30**.

A luma waveform hides single-channel clipping, so check the parade for a clipped red sunset or a blown stage light ([Kdenlive docs](https://docs.kdenlive.org/en/_sources/tips_and_tricks/scopes/waveform_and_rgb_parade.rst.txt)). Not every shot needs a 100% white: white paper "might be between 85–90 IRE" so it reads white without glowing ([PremiumBeat](https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/color-grading-quick-tip-non-absolute-white-and-black-points/)). For broadcast, EBU R103 expects an 8-bit video range of **16–235**, prefers values within **5–246**, and allows a total signal range of 1–254 ([EBU R103](https://tech.ebu.ch/publications/r103)). Web delivery should export legal range without hard clipping. A general Rec.709 guide places skin at **45–70 IRE**, depending on tone and lighting ([MediaGear](https://mediagear.ee/?p=421470)).

### Skin hue is universal, but luminance is not

All complexions share one hue: Adobe's vectorscope skin line "appears for all people regardless of their skin color" ([Adobe](https://www.adobe.com/learn/premiere-pro/web/correct-skin-tones)). Luminance and saturation differ. Alexis Van Hurkman's table, reproduced by Larry Jordan as "guidelines, not absolute rules," is the most-quoted reference ([Larry Jordan](https://larryjordan.com/articles/the-secret-to-setting-skin-colors-accurately/)):

| Complexion (Van Hurkman, via Larry Jordan) | Luma | Hue vs skin line | Saturation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Female Caucasian | 50–70% | +2° | 40% |
| Male Caucasian | 45–65% | +2° | 35% |
| Female Asian | 40–60% | −2° | 35% |
| Male Asian | 35–50% | −2° | 30% |
| Female Hispanic | 35–50% | +2° | 35% |
| Male Hispanic | 30–45% | +2° | 30% |
| Female Black | 15–35% | +2° | 20% |
| Male Black | 15–35% | +2° | 15% |

The table is contested. Another Larry Jordan article gives *maximum skin highlights* of 25–50% for Black women, which probably measures the brightest skin rather than the average ([Larry Jordan](https://larryjordan.com/articles/color-correction-make-people-look-normal/)). A practitioner calls the older tables "euro-centric." For lit Rec.709 footage they work to 60–65 IRE for the lightest skin and 45–50 IRE for the darkest, while noting that some measured dark skin sits at 18–29 IRE ([EOSHD forum](https://www.eoshd.com/comments/topic/49416-what-are-the-correct-skintone-values-in-ire-for-hlg-acquisition/)).

The cinematographers who shoot Black skin best reject lifting it to a fixed level:

- **Ava Berkofsky** (*Insecure*) rejects exposing dark skin around 70 IRE: pushing skin "up to 50 or 70 is just going to make the rest of the image look weird." She prefers bounced light and reflective makeup so "the skin can reflect it," and holds that "there's no formula" ([SLR Lounge](https://slrlounge.com/ava-berkofsky-of-hbos-insecure-on-how-to-light-dark-skin)).
- **Bradford Young** (*Selma*) deliberately limits light on faces to bring out "the rich tones and textures of their skin" ([The Stranger](https://www.thestranger.com/film/2015/01/14/21456854/cinematographer-bradford-young-illuminates-the-color-below-the-color-of-selma-and-a-most-violent-year)).
- **On *Moonlight*,** colourist Alex Bickel drew out "a rainbow of rich beautiful skin tones" for DP James Laxton, who wanted skin warm rather than harsh ([Post Magazine](https://www.postmagazine.com/Publications/Post-Magazine/2017/March-1-2017/Coloring-Best-Picture-winner-i-Moonlight-i-.aspx)). Bickel reportedly used Fuji, Agfa and Kodak emulations for the three life chapters ([Filmmaker](https://filmmakermagazine.com/?p=100603)).

The main failure is a teal shadow grade that drains warmth from darker complexions and leaves them ashy. The fix is to keep warmth in the skin while cooling the rest of the frame ([Toolfarm](https://www.toolfarm.com/tutorial/achieve-perfect-skin-tones-when-color-grading/)). A single low-authority blog also reports generic LUTs turning darker faces "orange, blotchy, or noisy" ([Passion Fuels Ambition](https://www.passionfuelsambition.com/dark-skin-tone-color-grading/)).

The agent rules that follow:

- Hold every face's hue on the skin line.
- Never raise whole-frame exposure to brighten dark skin; it flattens the scene and greys the face.
- Add presence through highlights or a skin-keyed secondary instead.
- Keep shadows dense.
- In multi-complexion scenes, check each face's vectorscope cluster separately.

### Log footage needs its conversion before any look

DJI's D-Log M "is not a LUT" but a flat, wide-dynamic-range profile ([Heliguy](https://heliguy.com/blogs/knowledge-base/what-is-the-dji-osmo-pocket-3-s-colour-mode-d-log-m/)). On the Osmo Pocket 3 it is the **10-bit** option, against the 8-bit Normal profile. A Pocket 3 settings guide recommends zebras at about **95%**, manual white balance (for example 5500K), and exposing slightly bright while keeping highlights unclipped ([OMG Adrian](https://omgadrian.beehiiv.com/p/the-only-osmo-pocket-3-settings-that-matter)). DJI publishes its own conversions ([DJI LUT downloads](https://www.dji.com/lut)):

| Camera | DJI conversion LUT |
|---|---|
| Osmo Pocket 3 | D-Log M to Rec.709 |
| Osmo Action 4 and Action 5 Pro | D-Log M to Rec.709 *vivid* |
| Mini 4 Pro, Air 3, Air 3S | D-Log M to Rec.709 |
| Mavic 3 | Separate D-Log M, D-Log and D-Log vivid conversions |

The workflow is: apply the matching LUT as Lumetri's Input LUT, set balance and exposure after it with small moves, then grade. A creative LUT should never go on raw log. Log footage is recognisable on the waveform: blacks float above 0%, bright scenes never reach 100%, and the vectorscope trace is short. Mixed-camera projects need each log source converted before shots are matched. Two points remain unresolved. Sources disagree on the Pocket 3's D-Log M base ISO (50, 400 or 800), and a LUT vendor claims DJI's LUT clips saturated colours, which is not independent evidence ([Gamut](https://gamut.io/camera/osmo-pocket-3/)).

### Named looks are recipes over a corrected base

Most named looks combine four levers: contrast-curve shape, black-point lift, split-toning and selective saturation.

- **Teal-orange.** Teal goes into the shadows and orange into the highlights, so skin pops against its complement. It needs foreground and background separation and fails on orange backgrounds ([PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2017/02/23/orange-teal-look-popular-hollywood/)). It is also widely called "far too overused" ([Fstoppers](https://www.fstoppers.com/lighting/how-balance-color-tones-blue-and-orange-look-587391)).
- **Bleach bypass.** Saturation drops to about **15–20 on Resolve's scale, where 50 is the default**, with an S-curve on top; the film original was often shot a stop under ([PremiumBeat](https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/color-grading-bleach-bypass-look-in-davinci-resolve/)).
- **Aged film in Lumetri.** Lower Highlights and raise Shadows, add a little Contrast, set Look Intensity to **120%** and **Faded Film to 20**, lower Saturation while raising Vibrance, tint shadows blue and highlights light orange, and finish with a vignette ([ProVideo Coalition](https://www.provideocoalition.com/a-closer-look-at-the-lumetri-color-panel/amp/)).
- **Film emulation.** There is "no one 'film look'" ([Mixing Light](https://mixinglight.com/color-tutorial/emulating-film-halation-in-davinci-resolve)). The common cues are deep but not crushed blacks, bright but not blown whites, grain most visible in the midtones, and red-orange halation around bright sources ([Imagen](https://imagen-ai.com/post/film-emulation-lightroom.md)).
- **Black and white.** Straight desaturation turns equal-luminance colours into "muddy gray." A channel mixer is better: skin lives in red, so raising red brightens skin and lowering it gives moodier, darker skin ([PremiumBeat](https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/using-the-channel-mixer-for-a-better-black-and-white)).
- **Vignette.** Start around Amount **−1.5**, Midpoint **40–60** and a soft feather ([Boris FX](https://borisfx.com/blog/how-to-add-a-video-vignette-effect-in-premiere-pro/)).

### White cycloramas clip on purpose, and 8-bit footage bands first

A cyc meant to read as infinite white is usually clipped deliberately, because an unclipped cyc shows its gradations and defects. One shooter found the background looked grey until it was lit just past 100 IRE, and that passed on a broadcast job ([Creative COW](https://creativecow.net/forums/thread/white-bg-no-waveform-monitor/)). Check the cyc on the parade so all three channels top out equally, rather than trusting the luma trace. Keep white wardrobe and paper lower, in the 85–90 IRE region, so they hold texture against the background ([PremiumBeat](https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/color-grading-quick-tip-non-absolute-white-and-black-points/)).

Bit depth caps how far any grade can push. 8-bit has 256 levels per channel against 1,024 for 10-bit. Pushing 8-bit too far bands skies and smooth walls and leaves "comb" gaps in the histogram ([DIY Photography](https://www.diyphotography.net/8-bit-vs-10-bit-editing/)). "8 bits for a 13 stop dynamic range is really not enough" ([Alister Chapman](https://alisterchapman.com/tag/bit)), which is why log is widely considered pointless without 10-bit. A near-white 8-bit gradient bands easily, so clipping a cyc cleanly is often safer than holding it just under white. Fine grain also acts as dither against banding.

## Dialogue anchors the mix, and −14 LUFS governs the web

Mixing starts with dialogue. Dialogue should peak in the **−12 to −6 dBFS** range and never touch 0 ([PLU](https://kb.plu.edu/100573); [Adorama](https://www.adorama.com/alc/mastering-audio-levels/)). Premiere's Essential Sound Auto-Match reportedly targets **dialogue −23 LUFS, music −25, SFX −21 and ambience −30**. Those values come from older courses and community posts, not a current Adobe page ([LinkedIn Learning](https://www.lynda.com/Premiere-Pro-tutorials/Using-Essential-Sound-panel-Loudness-dynamics/758641/2215703-4.html)). The figures for music under speech converge:

- WCAG 1.4.7's accessibility benchmark puts background sound at least **20 dB below** speech ([WCAG 1.4.7](https://www.disabilityworld.org/toolkit/standards/wcag/1-4-7-low-or-no-background-audio/)).
- Tutorials suggest −18 to −25 dB under the voice, rising to about −10 dB in gaps ([Pixflow](https://pixflow.net/blog/?p=92478)).
- Another guide puts it at 15–20 dB under ([IRPR](https://sounddesign.irpr.agency/guides/how-to-mix-audio-for-youtube/)).
- One documentary editor works at about 12 dB under ([Adobe Community](https://community.adobe.com/t5/premiere-pro-discussions/help-with-audio-settings-for-voice-over-and-music/m-p/12039621/highlight/true)).

The mixing order:

1. Level dialogue clips with clip gain.
2. Fill gaps with room tone.
3. Set the music bed.
4. Duck the music under speech.
5. Add SFX.
6. Compress the dialogue bus and limit the master.
7. Measure integrated loudness and true peak, adjust master gain, and re-measure.

| Destination | Integrated loudness | True-peak ceiling | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| EBU R128 broadcast | −23.0 LUFS ±0.5 LU | −1 dBTP | [EBU R128](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/r/r128-2014.pdf) |
| US ATSC A/85 (CALM Act) | −24 LKFS ±2 dB | −2 dBTP | [ProVideo Coalition](https://www.provideocoalition.com/the-calm-act-commercial-advertisement-loudness-mitigation/) |
| Netflix | −27 LKFS dialogue-gated ±2 LU | −2 dBTP | [Netflix Sound Mix Spec v1.5](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/360001794307-Netflix-Sound-Mix-Specifications-Best-Practices-v1-5) |
| Spotify | ~−14 LUFS | −1 dBTP (−2 if louder than −14) | [Spotify](https://support.spotify.com/artists/article/loudness-normalization/) |
| YouTube (measured, unpublished) | ~−14 LUFS reference; loud content turned down, quiet content not raised | — | [MeterPlugs / Ian Shepherd](https://meterplugs.com/blog/2019/09/18/youtube-changes-loudness-reference-to-14-lufs.html) |
| Apple Podcasts | −16 LKFS ±1 dB | −1 dBFS | [Apple](https://podcasters.apple.com/support/audio-requirements) |
| TikTok, Instagram, Facebook | no official figure; vendors claim ~−14 | — | [OpenClip](https://openclip.app/learn/audio-normalization) |

R128 was revised to V5 in November 2023 ([EBU](https://tech-qual.ebu.ch/files/live/sites/tech/files/shared/r/r128v5_0.pdf)). Loudness meters use a 0.4 s momentary window and a 3 s short-term window, and integrated loudness is gated at an absolute −70 LUFS and a relative −10 LU ([EBU Tech 3341](https://tech.ebu.ch/docs/tech/tech3341.pdf)).

The safe social-video export is **−14 LUFS integrated at −1 dBTP**. Mixing quieter just sounds weak on YouTube, which does not raise quiet uploads ([Youlean](https://youlean.co/?p=30813)). If FFmpeg renders the master:

- **Defaults.** `loudnorm` defaults to I = −24, LRA = 7 and TP = −2.
- **Linear mode.** Linear normalisation requires measured values from a first pass ([FFmpeg](https://ffmpeg.org/pipermail/ffmpeg-cvslog/2016-May/100083.html)). It silently falls back to dynamic compression if the target LRA or true peak can't be honoured ([ffmpeg-user](https://ffmpeg.org/pipermail/ffmpeg-user/2022-March/054422.html)).
- **Sample rate.** The filter upsamples internally to 192 kHz, so set `-ar 48000` explicitly ([AnimeThemes wiki](https://animethemes.moe/wiki/encoding/audio_normalization)).

### Every audio edit needs a fade, room tone and a downbeat

Clicks are prevented the same way everywhere. Snap edits to zero crossings, then crossfade if a click remains ([Sound On Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/techniques/removing-clicks-crossfades-sonar)). Audio dissolves at dialogue edits are "usually … pretty short, either a 1 or 2 frame duration" ([Film Editing Pro](https://www.filmeditingpro.com/?p=24342)), and VEGAS's default 10 ms quick-fade prevents clicks at frame boundaries ([VEGAS forum](https://www.vegascreativesoftware.info/us/forum/auto-crossfades--83076)). Long fades over every pop dull consonants, so check that a fade hasn't swallowed the first consonant ([DaVinci Resolve Club](https://davinciresolveclub.com/fix-audio-clicks-pops-davinci-resolve/)).

Room tone should be recorded on set (at least a couple of minutes) and scaled to the scene: quieter for intimate moments, louder for wides. Breaths should be lowered rather than deleted ([Production Expert](https://www.production-expert.com/production-expert-1/5-techniques-for-dialogue-editing-in-film-and-tv)). Room tone fills the gaps; it does not sit under dialogue, or the room grows louder whenever someone speaks ([Videomaker](https://www.videomaker.com/article/c3/14246-audio-for-video-using-radio-edit-techniques/)). Music cuts go "right before the '1' beat, ideally after a full phrase," never mid-beat or mid-chord ([Artlist](https://artlist.io/blog/how-to-add-music-to-a-video/)). In montages, cut picture every 2 or 4 beats rather than on every one, and use a reverb tail or fade to hide an awkward music edit ([RouteNote](https://licensing.routenote.com/blog/?p=9560)).

Ducking works in two ways:

- **Premiere auto-ducking.** It lives on clips tagged Music, with Duck Against, Sensitivity, Reduce By and Fades parameters. Adobe's example uses **800 ms** fades, and setting Sensitivity too high ducks nearly the whole clip ([Adobe](https://www.adobe.com/learn/premiere-pro/web/automatic-audio-ducking)).
- **Sidechain compression.** About **4:1** gives a typical music-under-speech duck, while 10:1 or more gives an obvious "radio" dip. An attack of 1–10 ms ducks instantly; 50–200 ms fades down more softly but lets the first syllable leak through ([Vindral](https://composer-docs.vindral.com/operators/operators-sidechain-ducking.html)).

A creator-tool starting preset is **6–10 dB reduction, 30–60 ms attack and 300–500 ms release**. Lengthen release to 400–700 ms if the music pumps, and move the duck earlier if the first word is buried ([CapCut](https://www.capcut.com/create/audio-ducking-for-clear-dialogue-in-video)). No primary film or TV source publishes ducking times or J-cut lead lengths. These are craft judgements.

The dialogue processing chain is consistent across sources:

1. **Targeted clean-up and noise reduction**, A/B tested against the original.
2. **High-pass filter** somewhere "between 50–100 Hz," without thinning the voice ([iZotope](https://www.izotope.com/community/blog/how-to-eq-vocals)).
3. **Corrective cuts** for nasal resonance around 800 Hz–1.5 kHz.
4. **Presence lift** around 2–5 kHz, using boosts of "no more than 1 or 2 dB."
5. **Gentle compression** at "2:1 or 3:1" with "a knee of 5 to 10 dB" ([Production Expert](https://www.production-expert.com/production-expert-1/5-techniques-for-dialogue-editing-in-film-and-tv)).
6. **De-essing**, typically at 5–10 kHz ([Kdenlive](https://docs.kdenlive.org/de/effects_and_filters/audio_effects/volume_and_dynamics/deesser.html)).
7. **A true-peak limiter** at the delivery ceiling.

Much production dialogue arrives already compressed, so check the source before adding more ([Gearspace](https://gearspace.com/threads/dialogue-compression.951277/)).

Sound design for short-form follows a few timing rules:

- **Whoosh.** "A whoosh has to match the move it covers, so length is everything." A short whoosh "peaking on the edit point carries the eye across it" ([Morphic](https://morphic.com/resources/sounds/whoosh-sound-effect)).
- **Reverse riser.** It "leads into a cut rather than covering one, so the silence after is the effect." Pairing a rush with a dull hit means "the transition arrives somewhere."
- **Shutter clicks.** Match the camera on screen: mirror slap for a DSLR, leaf blade, or a phone click. Sync clicks to the frame change and keep them under the music and dialogue. A rapid run of clicks can carry the rhythm of a slideshow or recap ([Morphic](https://morphic.com/resources/sounds/camera-shutter-sound-effect)).
- **Layering.** Recorded camera audio rarely delivers impact, so designers layer many effects ([DIY Photography](https://www.diyphotography.net/slow-motion-sound-in-videos-is-fake-well-most-of-it-is/)).

## Text lives inside 90% of the frame and stays up long enough to read twice

Safe areas are the one hard typographic standard. **EBU R95** (v1.1, 2017) sets a 3.5% action-safe margin and a 5% graphics-safe margin on every edge, leaving 93% and 90% of the frame ([EBU R95](https://tech.ebu.ch/publications/r095)). SMPTE uses the same 93%/90% pair, and the legacy 80% title-safe area still governs CEA-708 captions ([Wikipedia: Safe area](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safe_area_%28television%29)). At 1920×1080 the arithmetic gives an action-safe box of about 1786×1004 px and a **graphics-safe box of 1728×972 px, with 96 px side and 54 px top and bottom margins**. Double those at UHD. A broadcaster spec confirms the bottom figure, specifying a 1920×240 lower-third creative area above a **54 px** bottom safe space ([YES TV](https://yestv.com/advertise-with-yes-tv-staging)).

No standard gives title size as a percentage of frame height. Brand guides use app-specific point sizes ([Northeastern](https://brand.northeastern.edu/design-and-experiences/design/videography/)), and Netflix sizes subtitles so that 42 characters fit across the screen ([Netflix TTSG](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/217350977-English-USA-Timed-Text-Style-Guide)).

Typography rules are craft consensus:

- **All-caps needs tracking.** Capitals are designed to sit next to lowercase, so set caps need added tracking and pair kerning, and all-caps should be kept for short titles ([Pimp My Type](https://pimpmytype.com/spacing-all-caps/)).
- **Two typefaces maximum.** Pair a characterful display face with a calm body face ([Miracamp](https://www.miracamp.com/learn/after-effects/create-cinematic-titles)).
- **Genre conventions.** Serifs such as Trajan suit epic and historical work. Geometric sans faces such as Futura suit modern work and are often tracked wide. Bold condensed sans suits action.
- **Slow tracking expansion** is a staple cinematic title. Premiere historically could not keyframe tracking, so it is built in After Effects or a MOGRT ([Adobe Community](https://community.adobe.com/t5/premiere-pro-discussions/animating-text-to-expand/m-p/13790782)).
- **Contrast.** Text should meet WCAG contrast of at least **4.5:1** for normal and 3:1 for large text ([Kineclip](https://kineclip.com/blog/caption-readability-checklist-short-videos-2026/)), using a plate, shadow or outline over busy footage.

On-screen duration follows reading speed. The "read-twice" rule holds a title long enough to read it twice: about **3 s for a ten-letter title plus 1 s per five more letters** ([Pinnacle](https://help.pinnaclesys.com/pinnacle/v24/en/help/pinnacle-studio/h2-giving-your-project-a-title.html)). Netflix caps adult subtitles at **20 characters per second** (17 for children) ([Netflix TTSG](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/217350977-English-USA-Timed-Text-Style-Guide)). BBC subtitles run 160–180 words per minute, with a minimum of roughly 0.3 s per word ([Clevercast](https://clevercast.com/bbc-subtitling-guidelines)). Short branded intro cards typically last 2–3 s ([Clueso](https://www.clueso.io/glossary/title-card)).

### Netflix's caption rules are the timing backbone

Netflix's style guides are the most complete public caption specification:

- **Line length:** 42 characters per line, at most two lines, kept on one line unless it exceeds 42. Breaks go after punctuation and before conjunctions, and never split article from noun or first name from last ([Netflix TTSG](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/217350977-English-USA-Timed-Text-Style-Guide)).
- **Duration:** a minimum of **20 frames** (5/6 s) and a maximum of **7 s** per event ([Netflix General Requirements](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/215758617-Timed-Text-Style-Guide-General-Requirements)).
- **Gaps:** at least **2 frames** between subtitles. A gap is either 2 frames or at least 0.5 s, so gaps under half a second close to 2 frames.
- **In-times:** on the first frame of audio. If dialogue starts within 0.5 s of a shot change, snap the in-time to the cut.
- **Out-times:** an out-time within 0.5 s before a cut extends to the cut minus 2 frames ([Netflix Timing Guidelines](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/360051554394-Timed-Text-Style-Guide-Subtitle-Timing-Guidelines)).

In frames, half a second is 12 at 24 fps, 15 at 30 and 30 at 60.

Vertical video needs shorter lines. Nimdzi recommends 1–2 lines of at most 37 characters ([Nimdzi](https://www.nimdzi.com/subtitling-vertical-videos-guidelines-where-art-thou/)), and one vendor finds about 20 characters works best ([Simon Says](https://www.simonsaysai.com/help/5520911-subtitles-in-vertical-videos)). A Reels guide puts text height at 60–75 px on a 1080×1920 frame and keeps captions **450–480 px above the bottom edge** ([Clipspeed](https://www.clipspeed.ai/blog/instagram-reels-captions.html)). Captions belong in the lower-middle centre, away from the right-side action rail and the bottom metadata ([Reap](https://reap.video/blog/short-form-video-safe-zones)). Burn stylised captions into short-form feeds and upload a clean SRT to YouTube and Vimeo ([convertaudiototext](https://convertaudiototext.com/blog/burning-subtitles-into-video)). Never do both on a platform that renders its own captions on top.

### One master export covers nearly every platform

YouTube's official upload recommendation doubles as a universal master ([YouTube Help 1722171](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1722171?hl=en)):

- **Container:** MP4 with no edit lists and the moov atom at the front (fast start).
- **Video:** H.264 High Profile, progressive, 2 consecutive B-frames, closed GOP of half the frame rate (12 frames at 24 fps, 15 at 30), CABAC, 4:2:0, BT.709.
- **Audio:** AAC-LC at 48 kHz, 384 kbps for stereo.
- **Frame rate:** upload at the recorded frame rate.

Every other platform varies only resolution, aspect ratio, bitrate and duration.

### Vertical safe zones and letterbox bars are arithmetic

Meta is the only platform that publishes numeric vertical safe zones. For Reels ads, keep the **top 14%, bottom 35% and 6% on each side** free of text and logos ([Meta Ads Guide](https://www.facebook.com/business/ads-guide/update/video/instagram-reels)). On 1080×1920 that means 269 px top, 672 px bottom and 65 px each side, leaving a safe box of about 950×979 px. TikTok supplies safe zones only as downloadable templates ([TikTok Ads](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/video-ads-specifications?lang=en)). Community measurements of YouTube Shorts put the right rail at up to **201 px** ([Adkit](https://adkit.so/tools/safe-zones/youtube)). The conservative cross-platform rule is to keep essentials roughly within x 110–880 and y 290–1250, with organic captions allowed lower. These figures change whenever an app's UI changes, so they belong in configurable presets.

Letterbox arithmetic uses active height = width ÷ ratio, with each bar taking half of the remaining height. Premiere's own help builds letterboxing from Motion position and scale ([Adobe Help](https://helpx.adobe.com/no/premiere/desktop/edit-projects/intro-to-editing/frame-aspect-ratio.html)).

| Target in 1920×1080 | Active picture | Bar each | Crop top/bottom |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.39:1 | 1920×803 | ≈138 px (UHD ≈276) | 12.8% |
| 2.35:1 | 1920×817 | ≈131 px | 12.2% |
| 2.00:1 | 1920×960 | 60 px | 5.6% |
| 1.85:1 | 1920×1038 | ≈21 px | 1.9% |
| 4:3 pillarbox | 1440×1080 | 240 px each side | — |

A 9:16 crop from a 1080p frame keeps only 607.5 px of width (31.6%) and needs a 1.78× upscale. From UHD the crop is 1215×2160 and downscales cleanly to 1080×1920, which is why vertical deliverables should be mastered from 4K. YouTube itself advises "avoid adding padding or black bars directly to your video." A PremiumBeat article therefore recommends exporting at the true ratio, for example 1920×817, instead of baking in PNG bars ([PremiumBeat](https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/png-overlays-youtube-uploads/)).

## Genre decides whether speech or music is the spine

The most transferable genre rule is a fork. If a genre carries meaningful speech, build the audio spine first: documentaries start as a "radio" cut of interviews ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2015/11/how-cut-short-documentary-films-part-two-building-story-adding-b-roll)), and wedding films choose words first and sync music last ([RoughCut](https://roughcut.com/blog/how-to-structure-wedding-highlight-film)). If it doesn't, lock the music or a temp sound direction before cutting. Fashion films and Casey Neistat both work that way ([Cyprus Mail](https://cyprus-mail.com/2026/08/10/seedance-2-0-for-fashion-moving-from-mood-board-to-campaign-film); [Artlist](https://artlist.io/blog/editing-storytelling-techniques-casey-neistat/)).

| Genre | Spine | Structural anchor | Signature rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Photoshoot BTS | Music | Process → finished stills; 1–4 min | Never boring; show the finals |
| Wedding highlight | Vows and speeches | Five acts; 8–15 soundbites for 7 min | Don't cut vows with speech audio |
| Documentary | Interviews | Hook, information, closure; three acts | Must work as audio alone |
| Music video | Song structure | Performance backbone plus B-roll | Vary lengths; shots ≈ one bar |
| Ads | Message | ABCD; hook → USPs → CTA | Brand early; voiced CTA |
| Fashion film | Temp sound | Mood and garment, not plot | Let fabric behave on screen |
| Travel | Music plus ambience | Ease in, build, ease out | Point of interest stays in place |
| Vlog / YouTube | Voice and title promise | Validate the promise in 60 s; escalate | New information every 10–15 s |
| Reels / TikTok / Shorts | Hook | Proposition in 3 s; hook → value → CTA | Safe zones; loopable end |
| Corporate / event | Testimony | Customer as hero; scene, core, takeaway | One problem, one change |

### Montage genres: BTS, fashion, travel and music video

**Photoshoot BTS.** Nikon puts the ideal photography BTS at "usually from one to four minutes." Coverage should run from wide to close-up across hair and make-up, wardrobe, gear, the photographer at work and the model interaction, plus a tripod time-lapse of the set build. The editor should "choose clips with the most emotion." Finished retouched images usually go at the end, and "the worst thing you can do is show a video of your shoot that looks boring" ([Nikon USA](https://www.nikonusa.com/en/learn-and-explore/a/tips-and-techniques/how-to-create-a-behind-the-scenes-video-for-your-photography-business.html)). Published work also uses alternating "shot vs result" pairs, each point-of-view clip followed by its retouched photo ([PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2015/06/12/bts-a-point-of-view-look-at-a-fashion-shoot-by-gerd-tschebular/)). The research team infers that on social platforms the hook rule moves a strobe-pop or shot-to-final pair into the first 3 s, rather than holding all payoffs to the end. The "flash moment" itself, a strobe frame plus a shutter click cut to the final still, is common creator practice with no written authority.

**Fashion films** are mood- and garment-led. A temp sound direction decides how quickly a turn happens. Structured coats hold their shape while light dresses respond to air, and accessories may only catch light during a turn, so they need their own moments. "Simply cropping the wide version may remove the gesture or garment detail," so design for each aspect ratio. Hem length, bag closure and jewellery side are the usual continuity slips ([Cyprus Mail](https://cyprus-mail.com/2026/08/10/seedance-2-0-for-fashion-moving-from-mood-board-to-campaign-film)). A useful checkpoint: if viewers can't read the caption, understand the product or feel the emotional change, the montage is too fast ([CapCut](https://www.capcut.com/create/music-video-pacing-emotional-montage-rhythm)).

**Travel films** should "start out slow, gently ease your audience into the experience," build, then ease back down ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/editing-techniques-better-travel-videos)). They keep the point of interest in the same area of frame across cuts, avoid cutting between moving and static shots (or add motion to the static one), and layer sound under the image, such as rain over grey clouds before the downpour. Coverage mixes establishing, detail and movement shots with ambient sound balanced against the music ([Splice](https://spliceapp.com/blog/airport-montage-editing-for-travel-creators/)). Shape, action and sound match cuts move a subject from location to location ([Backstage](https://www.backstage.com/magazine/article/what-is-a-match-cut-75670/)).

**Music videos** start from the song map. Choose a performance-led, narrative-led or hybrid approach ([Hedra](https://www.hedra.com/docs/pages/app/content-creation/creating-music-videos)). Mark beats, cut fast for energy and use longer takes for emotional passages ([Pixflow](https://pixflow.net/blog/how-to-edit-a-music-video-in-premiere-pro/)), avoid cutting on every beat, and remember that dominant shot lengths cluster at one bar ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.00970)). From that the research team derives an energy map, which no source states directly: verses cut every one or two bars, choruses every half-bar to every beat, bridges break the pattern, and the final chorus gets the densest cutting and the best shots.

### Speech genres: wedding, documentary, vlog and corporate

**Wedding highlights** have the most explicit published structure. RoughCut's seven-minute arc ([RoughCut](https://roughcut.com/blog/how-to-structure-wedding-highlight-film)) runs:

| Act | Time (7 min film) | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | 0:00–1:00 | One universal line over atmospheric B-roll; not vows, a joke or a long speech |
| Build | 1:00–3:00 | Speeches telling the love story, 2–3 speakers |
| Climax | 3:00–5:00 | Vows, rings, kiss; the music peaks |
| Resolution | 5:00–6:30 | Lighter energy; don't match the climax |
| Closing | 6:30–7:00 | One strong line, then fade to black |

It uses **8–15 soundbites, not 30**, and applies a cut test: "If you can remove a clip and the story still works, remove it." Scaled proportionally to a four-minute highlight, the act breaks fall at about 0:34, 1:43, 2:51 and 3:43. PremiumBeat disagrees on the opening and suggests a speech or vows to introduce the couple. It also advises against chronological order, often uses two songs (slow for ceremony, faster for reception), pans in the same direction across a dissolve, uses light leaks sparingly, and keeps the highlight well under 10 minutes ([PremiumBeat](https://www.premiumbeat.com/blog/5-tips-editing-cinematic-wedding-highlight-videos)). Instagram favours several short 9:16 clips of 10–15 s over one long teaser ([Maroo](https://www.maroo.us/blogs/6-tips-to-using-tiktok-reels-for-wedding-pros)). Engagement and couple films follow the same emotional logic without vows.

**Documentaries** begin with an interview-only assembly that ignores visuals and is tested as audio alone, then add B-roll in a basic three-act structure ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2015/11/how-cut-short-documentary-films-part-two-building-story-adding-b-roll)). The arc is that of an essay: hook, new information, closure ([Indiana University](https://jkmedia.sitehost.iu.edu/articles/interview_tips.shtml)). B-roll illustrates, hides jump cuts and breaks up talking heads ([Adobe](https://www.adobe.com/creativecloud/video/discover/b-roll)). Stillmotion's MUSE process frames each film around People, Place, Purpose and Plot ([ProVideo Coalition](https://www.provideocoalition.com/muse-a-storytelling-tool/)). Short-form micro-docs must be narrow, with one problem and one transformation ([CapCut](https://www.capcut.com/create/micro-documentary-short-form-video)). The tightening question is "How many sentences or words can I take out, and still tell the same story?" ([Todd Boruff](https://toddboruff.substack.com/p/my-interview-editing-process)).

**YouTube long-form** is organised around retention. MrBeast's leaked production guide, confirmed authentic by former staff, teases the best moments in the first 60 s and adds a twist or escalation roughly every three minutes ([OMR](https://omr.com/de/daily/mrbeast-doc-pdf-leak); [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2024/09/26/youtube-mrbeast-jimmy-donaldson-leaked-business-handbook-advice)). Joos's rule of new information every 10–15 s sets the micro-cadence ([Vidpros](https://vidpros.com/retention-editing-what-you-can-learn-from-mario-joos/)). Neistat's grammar is jump cuts that consolidate a talking head, cutting off mid-sentence once the audience knows how the thought ends, whip pans cut on the blur, and time-lapses that skip dead time ([Artlist](https://artlist.io/blog/editing-storytelling-techniques-casey-neistat/)).

**Short-form vertical.** Reels and Shorts now allow 3 minutes. Still, 46% of 507 creators surveyed named under 30 s as ideal ([Adobe Express](https://www.adobe.com/express/learn/blog/youtube-shorts-length-study)), and YouTube's creator liaison says a Short should be as long as it needs to be and no longer ([Piktochart](https://piktochart.com/blog/how-long-youtube-shorts.md)).

**Corporate brand stories** make "your customer… the hero. Not your brand" ([Cognitive Films](https://www.cognitivefilms.com/customer-hero-brand-story/)) and run a three-act arc from backstory through struggle to transformation ([The Story of Telling](https://thestoryoftelling.com/three-act-structure-brand-stories/)). Event recaps open with a scene-setter, carry the core content chronologically and end on a clear takeaway ([Canon](https://cinemaeos.canon.ca/?p=3005)). They are cut as a cinematic version plus 30–60 s social reels ([PlayPlay](https://playplay.com/blog/event-highlights-video/)).

**Ads** follow Google's ABCD framework:

- **Attention:** get "to the heart of the story faster" with tight framing.
- **Branding:** brand "from the start," reinforced by audio brand mentions.
- **Connection:** feature people, with one focused message.
- **Direction:** a CTA reinforced "with voice-over."

([Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/14783551)). Six-second bumpers carry one message, open on a striking image and give the closing thought about 2 s. Trimming a 30 s spot down to a bumper tends to cram the close ([Think with Google](https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/intl/en-gb/marketing-strategies/video/youtube-bumper-ads-best-practices-video-editing)). Cutdowns should therefore be re-edited, not trimmed. A 30 s spot's problem-to-solution turn should "feel earned, so let each beat breathe" ([Morphic](https://morphic.com/resources/videos/30-second-commercial-videos)).

## Premium means one motivated move, built precisely

Professional-looking effects follow the same pattern: the mechanism is simple, the timing is exact, and the use is scarce. What reads as dated is the reverse: star wipes, page curls and glitter flares ([Film Editing Pro](https://www.filmeditingpro.com/?p=24508)), a light leak on every cut, heavy teal-orange, and baked-in PNG letterbox bars. 2026 trend writing points toward "light color correction instead of heavy filters," captions on everything, and voice-led storytelling ([Clipchamp](https://clipchamp.com/en/blog/video-trends-2025/)). The guiding principle is "more is less" ([Synthesia](https://synthesia.io/post/video-transitions)).

**Aspect ratio for emphasis.** Static bars are a Crop of 12.8% top and bottom for 2.39:1, from the table above. The strongest professional use is a *motivated change* of aspect. Xavier Dolan's *Mommy* runs almost entirely in 1:1 and opens to widescreen only for its two happiest moments ([RogerEbert.com](https://www.rogerebert.com/reviews/mommy-2015)). When Netflix UK streamed it without the expansion, Dolan said it "shut down the emotional capacity of that scene" ([CP24](https://www.cp24.com/entertainment-news/movie-tv-news/director-dolan-lashes-out-at-netflix-over-screening-format-of-mommy-1.2723136)). The effect depends on happening once or twice.

**Flashes.** A camera-flash pop rises fast and falls slowly: dip to white over "just 2 or 3 frames, then the dissolve back… maybe 20 frames" ([DVinfo](https://www.dvinfo.net/forum/archive/index.php/t-104689.html)). A 2-frame pure-white flash "looks like lightning" ([VEGAS forum](https://www.vegascreativesoftware.info/us/forum/camera-flash-effect--93391)). Trailer houses use 1–2 frames of solid white, or a cut to white followed by a 3-frame fade ([Adobe Community](https://community.adobe.com/t5/premiere-pro/video-effects-for-a-horror-thriller-trailer/m-p/5798185)). Flashes should signal something, such as a photo, an intrusive memory or a hidden threat, rather than decorate ([Melies](https://melies.co/cinematic-techniques/editing/flash-cut)). Safety is non-negotiable here: WCAG 2.3.1 forbids **more than three flashes in any one-second period** above the luminance thresholds ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG20/Understanding/three-flashes-or-below-threshold)). At 24 fps that means at least 8 frames between full-frame flashes.

**Whips, zooms and hidden cuts.** Tutvid's synthetic whip pan:

1. Put an adjustment layer over the cut, from 5 frames before to 10 after.
2. Animate Offset's "Shift Center To" X from 960 to −960.
3. Key Directional Blur at 90°, length **250 at the exact cut frame** and 0 at the ends.
4. Ease out the first keys and ease in the last.

([Tutvid](https://tutvid.com/premiere-pro/whip-pan-blurring-transition-effect-premiere-pro/)). In-camera whips still look better. Pan both shots the same direction at similar speed, match brightness at the whip points, and trim A where the blur begins and B where it ends ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/2017/09/5-steps-create-perfect-whip-pan-transition)). Zoom transitions use the Transform effect, Scale **100 → 300**, with "Use Composition's Shutter Angle" unchecked and a shutter of 180–360°. Fill the edges with Replicate and Mirror, and shape the velocity curve so speed peaks at the cut ([Boris FX](https://borisfx.com/blog/how-to-do-a-zoom-transition-in-premiere-pro-4-steps/)). Whips suit action, time jumps and comedic reveals but become gimmicks when overused ([Nikon](https://www.nikon.co.uk/en_GB/learn-and-explore/magazine/tips-and-tricks/cut-to-the-chase-10-tricks-to-add-pace-and-energy-to-your-edits)). Zooms between spaces with no spatial relationship cause dizziness ([Editframe](https://editframe.com/skills/brand-video-generator/transition-styles)). Outfit-change match cuts lock the camera, mark the floor, and cut at the motion peak "when the movement is blurriest" ([Planoly](https://www.planoly.com/blog/easy-reels-tiktok-transitions)).

**Push-ins, shake and split screens.** A subtle push-in scales "105% to 110%" over the shot, depending on picture and duration ([Adobe Community](https://community.adobe.com/t5/premiere-pro-discussions/how-do-i-get-the-ken-burns-effect-on-premiere-pro-cc-2015/m-p/8006633)). A very slow push near the end of an emotional piece draws the viewer in ([PremiumBeat](https://premiumbeat.com/blog/ken-burns-effect-premiere-pro/)). The most natural fake handheld borrows real shake: Warp Stabilizer is run in reverse from a real handheld reference clip ([No Film School](https://nofilmschool.com/add-handheld-camera-shake-premiere-pro)). Split screens crop each panel to keep 100/n percent of the width, so a triptych keeps 33.3% of each clip ([Motion Array](https://motionarray.com/premiere-pro/split-screen-adobe-premiere-pro-tutorial/)).

**Film texture belongs below conscious notice.**

- **Grain.** Use 15–30% for a subtle look ([Vindral](https://composer.docs.vindral.com/docs/film-grain)), kept out of the brightest highlights and deepest shadows, where it looks pasted on ([Gamut](https://gamut.io/best-film-grain-halation-comparison/)). Colourists are split here: Cullen Kelly is quoted as avoiding grain for most of 2022 ([Daejeon Chronicles](https://daejeonchronicles.com/2022/12/26/no-love-for-film-grain)).
- **Halation.** It clings to bright sources and high-contrast edges. Dehancer advises starting strong and reducing ([Dehancer](https://www.dehancer.com/learn/article/halation)). A plugin-free Premiere version blurs the red channel of a duplicate at about 50 ([Miracamp](https://miracamp.com/post/film-halation-effect-in-premiere-pro)).
- **Gate weave.** Works at fractional-pixel amplitude, with frei0r defaulting to 0.2 ([frei0r](https://gstreamer.freedesktop.org/documentation/frei0r/frei0r-filter-gate-weave.html)).
- **Light leaks.** Use Screen blend with the leak's brightest frame on the cut ([Tutvid](https://tutvid.com/premiere-pro/create-light-leak-transition-premiere-pro/)).
- **Vignette.** Viewers should feel it without noticing it ([Boris FX](https://borisfx.com/blog/how-to-add-a-video-vignette-effect-in-premiere-pro/)).

**Sound-led and abrupt cuts.** J/L offsets have no standard. One automation tool defaults to "1–10 frames," and other guides say to adjust by feel ([FireCut](https://firecut.ai/blog/mastering-the-split-edit-how-j-cuts-and-l-cuts-work/)). Title reveals use Track Matte Key with text as the matte, or animated crops and masks ([Motion Array](https://motionarray.com/learn/premiere-pro/adobe-premiere-pro-text-effects/)). Smash cuts, including a smash to black and silence, work only when unprepared and rare ([StudioBinder](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/what-is-a-smash-cut-definition/)).

## Great editors read notes as symptoms, not prescriptions

Judging your own cut is mostly about regaining fresh eyes. Frame.io's screening advice:

- **Timing.** Perspective typically goes **3–5 weeks** into an edit.
- **Self-review.** Take a day off, then watch the whole cut without pausing, re-cutting or taking notes.
- **Prep.** Level the sound first, because "bad sound is so distracting that it often can ruin a regular screening."
- **Audience.** **4–10 people**, weighted toward the target audience and light on industry viewers, who nitpick.
- **Questions.** Ask general questions before scene-specific ones, so you don't steer opinion.

([Frame.io](https://blog.frame.io/2017/07/06/successful-test-screening-tips/)). Walker values weekends away, judges scenes with the sound off, keeps an imaginary audience in mind ("what are they wondering, has the film answered it?"), and late in the process flips the image and turns it black-and-white so it feels newly seen. Doing that too early "blow[s] it" ([Frame.io](https://blog.frame.io/2021/10/27/art-of-the-cut-dune-joe-walker/)).

Notes map to symptoms ([Frame.io](https://blog.frame.io/2017/07/06/successful-test-screening-tips/)):

- **"Bored"** can mean pacing, or missing stakes or motivation. Viewers who care about the characters will tolerate slow moments.
- **"Confused"** means the story or performances aren't carrying the stakes.
- **"It didn't hook me"** usually points to a weak first act.

When several people raise the same issue, treat it as real "even if their proposed fix is wrong." Take seriously any note that confirms a scene you already doubted. Check your ego and be willing to cut loved scenes, jokes or characters. Notes arrive vague, political or contradictory ([HowToFilmSchool](https://howtofilmschool.com/dictionary/notes-session/)). The editor's job is to diagnose the cause, not to carry out the suggestion literally.

Length problems are usually solved by shaving, not amputation. Thelma Schoonmaker's four-hour *Wolf of Wall Street* reached its running time by "shav[ing] things down" over three or four screenings, because removing whole scenes would have been "devastating" ([The Playlist](https://theplaylist.net/thelma-schoonmaker-says-2-part-4-hour-wolf-of-wall-street-was-considered-plus-watch-1-hour-charlie-rose-talk-20131223/)). Walker warns that easy cuts exist, "but you would have lost a lot," and leaves scenes that already work untouched; one *Dune* knife fight stayed frame-accurate to a cut made two days after the shoot ([Frame.io](https://blog.frame.io/2021/10/27/art-of-the-cut-dune-joe-walker/)).

For online video the audience's verdict is measurable. YouTube's dips mark abandonment, spikes mark rewatches (which can also mean a section was unclear), and late "top moments" should move earlier ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en)). Typical retention is benchmarked against the channel's own last ten videos of similar length ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1715160)). That gives an agent a feedback signal a human test screening cannot match.

## Conclusion

The structure of this knowledge suggests how to build the agent. The compliance layer is automatable and pass/fail: loudness and true peak, scope legality, skin-line hue, safe-zone and caption-rate checks, conform and frame-rate math, and flash-frequency safety. Those belong in validators that run on every render, not in prompts. The judgement layer cannot be scored shot by shot. Kuleshov's replications and the finding that music alone changes how a face is read mean any evaluation of a reaction shot, a hold or a cut point has to look at the sequence around it. Murch's same-frame-three-times test and Hecht and Kalkofen's huge tolerance window both say the same thing: a good cut point is a stable region, not a single exact frame. An agent should search for robust cut regions and break ties by emotion, rather than chase frame-perfect continuity.

The weakest evidence sits exactly where an automated editor will act most often. No authoritative source fixes J/L lead times, beat offsets, ramp durations, freeze holds, flash lengths beyond forum consensus, grade slider values, or organic vertical safe zones. TikTok and Instagram publish no loudness target at all. These should ship as named, adjustable parameters with the sourced anchors from this report as defaults, then be tuned by A/B testing on real footage and by platform retention data. Two defaults carry ethical weight beyond taste. Keep darker skin dense and warm instead of normalising it upward, and keep any flashing to three or fewer per second. Across almost every technique, the ingredient that separates premium from amateur work is a budget, not a better effect: one aspect change, one freeze device, a few slow-motion peaks and one fade to black per structural break.
